\begin{abstract}
The author has developed an algorithm
for finding exact, non-sep\-arable solutions of partial differential equations
and of systems of them.
The algorithm uses differential Thomas decompositon, Ritt's full reduction algorithm \cite{Ritt} and the GTZ algorithm \cite{GTZ}
to find ODEs that solve the system.  We prove a completeness theorem for the
core algorithm, and demonstrate how to use the algorithm
to solve hydrogen's Schr\"odinger equation.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
%% \subsection*{Differential Algebra}

Starting with the following Schr\"odinger equation for hydrogen \cite{pauling and wilson}:

\begin{equation}
\label{schrodinger hydrogen}
-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 \Psi - \frac{1}{r}\Psi = E \Psi
\end{equation}

We can require the solution to solve the following parameterized ODE:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
(a_0 + a_1 v) \frac{d^2\Psi(v)}{dv^2} + (b_0 + b_1 v) \frac{d\Psi(v)}{dv} + (c_0 + c_1 v) \Psi(v) = 0
\end{gathered}
\label{ansatz 1a}
\end{equation}

where $\Psi(v)$ is a univariate function of some variable $v$, itself expressed in a parameterized form:

\begin{equation}
v = v_1 x + v_2 y + v_3 z + v_4 r
\label{ansatz 1b}
\end{equation}

and expect to find that for certain constant values:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
a_1 = v_4 = 1 \qquad
b_0 = c_0 = 2 \qquad
c_1 = E \\
a_0 = b_1 = v_1 = v_2 = v_3 = 0
\end{gathered}
\label{ansatz 1sol}
\end{equation}

we will obtain the classical radial equation for hydrogen:

\begin{equation}
% \label{classical eq in ideal}
\begin{gathered}
r \frac{d^2\Psi(r)}{dr^2} + 2 \frac{d\Psi(r)}{dr} + 2(1 + E r) \Psi(r) = 0
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

a result more commonly derived using separation of variables.  Solving the radial equation
yields the ground state solution of hydrogen:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\Psi(r)=e^{-r} \\
E=-1/2
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

What might not be expected is to find a different set of constant values:

\begin{equation}
\label{J0 sol}
\begin{gathered}
E = 0 \\
a_0 = 0 \qquad
a_1 = 1 \\
b_0 = -1 \qquad
b_1 = 0 \\
c_0 = -1 \qquad
c_1 = 0 \\
v_1 = 1 \qquad
v_2 = 0 \qquad
v_3 = 0 \qquad
v_4 = 1
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

that produce a different ODE:

\begin{equation}
v \frac{d^2 \Psi}{dv}(v) + \frac{d\Psi}{dv}(v) + \Psi(v) = 0
\end{equation}

which can be solved to find a different solution to \eqref{schrodinger hydrogen}:

\begin{equation}
\Psi = J_0(2\sqrt{x+r})
\end{equation}

where $x,y,z$ are Cartesian coordinates, $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, $E=0$,
and $J_0$ is the ordinary Bessel function $J_0$.  This solution is not of the
familiar spherical separated form $R(r)\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\varphi)$, nor is it
Cartesian-separable; it is, however, separable in parabolic coordinates, a
reflection of the hydrogen atom's hidden symmetry that we take up in
\S\ref{sec:Example}.

The author has developed an algorithm that tests a system of differential
equations, specified as a finite set of differential polynomials,
against a second such set (which I term an ``ansatz''),
parameterized by a finite number of constants,
similar to \eqref{ansatz 1a} and \eqref{ansatz 1b},
and finds solutions like \eqref{ansatz 1sol} and \eqref{J0 sol}.  This is done using the techniques of differential
algebra and algebraic geometry.  The restriction to differential
polynomials is not severe; most elementary and special functions
can be easily modeled, as will be shown below.  Nothing in the development
requires the system to consist of partial differential equations, nor to
contain more than one of them: a single PDE is the case treated in
\S\ref{sec:Example}, a system of four in \S\ref{sec:NavierStokes}.

In short, to solve the system, we restrict our attention to a subset of the function
space, parameterized by a finite number of constants
and described using differential polynomials.
A quick glance at Section \ref{sec:Ansatze} should indicate the great variety of such ``ans\"atze''.
If we can, by luck or by theory, pick a good ansatz, then we have
a computable algorithm to find the solutions therein.

\section{The Ansatz}
\label{sec:Ansatze}

The primary intent of the algorithm is find solutions to a PDE that can be constructed using ODEs.

We do this by introducing additional differential polynomials, parameterized with constants,
forming what we term the ``ansatz''.

Using differential polynomials means that we can't restrict our functions to
something like $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, because that requires imposing a global integral condition,
and there's no way to specify that using only differential polynomials.
Nor can we specify any kind of boundary condition.

We can, however, restrict the function space by requiring the solutions
to satisfy additional differential equations.

\subsection{Differential Polynomials}

J.F. Ritt introduced differential algebra \cite{Ritt}.
Starting with a ring $R$ or a field $F$, we can construct a
{\it differential ring} or a {\it differential field}
by specifying one or more derivations, unary operators that
% commute with addition and obey the Leibniz rule for multiplication:
obey two additional axioms:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{gathered}
dx(a+b) = dx(a) + dx(b) \\
dx(ab) = (dx(a))b + a(dx(b))
\end{gathered}
\end{equation*}

A differential ring (resp. field) equipped with a single derivation
is called an {\it ordinary} differential ring; one
equipped with multiple derivations is called a {\it partial}
differential ring.

Various notations are in common use for the derivations;
since we work mostly in partial differential rings where there are multiple
derivations, we refer to derivations by prefixing
them with a $d$, i.e, $dx$.  We follow Kolchin (\cite{Kolchin} \S I.1)
and further assume that all derivations are pairwise commutative:

\begin{equation*}
dx(dy(a)) = dy(dx(a))
\end{equation*}

Let $\Theta$ be the free abelian monoid generated by the derivations;
we call the elements of $\Theta$ the {\it derivative operators}, and they act on
the differential ring by repeated application of the derivations.
The identity element of $\Theta$ acts as the identity mapping on the differential ring.
The {\it order} of a differential operator (${\rm ord}\ \delta$) is the number of derivations used to construct it.

A derivative operator applied to an indeterminate is called a {\it derivative},
and we refer to the indeterminates and their derivatives collectively as {\it differential variables}.
We generally use ``jet notation'' for derivatives,
where the applied derivations
are denoted by subscripted letters on the indeterminates, i.e, $\Psi_x$
is the result of applying derivation $dx$ to $\Psi$.

It is convenient to
let each indeterminate introduce an infinite set of derivatives by pairing
the indeterminate with all the elements of $\Theta$, so introducing $\Psi$
also introduces $\Psi_x$, $\Psi_y$, $\Psi_{xy}$, $\Psi_{xxx}$, etc.

For each derivation, there will typically exist in the differential ring an associated indeterminate,
labeled with the same letter,
with the property that applying a derivation to its
associated indeterminate produces unity, i.e, $x_x = 1$.  Applying a derivation to
the associated indeterminate of a different derivation produces zero, i.e, $y_x = 0$.
Sometimes it is important to remember that while the same letter is used for both
$x$ and $dx$, they are distinct objects. $x$ is an element of a differential
ring, while $dx$ is a unary operator defined on a differential ring.

Ritt's convention, which we follow, is to write a differential ring by stating
its coefficient ring and then listing the {\it differential indeterminates} ---
the dependent variables --- in curly braces: $\mathfrak{F}\{y_1, \ldots, y_n\}$
is the totality of differential polynomials in $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ over
$\mathfrak{F}$ (\cite{Ritt}, \S3).  The braces do {\it not} list the derivations,
which are separate data; we write $\Delta = \{\delta_1, \ldots, \delta_m\}$ for
them, and $\Theta$ for the free commutative monoid they generate, so that the
elements $\theta y_i$ with $\theta \in \Theta$ are the {\it derivatives} of the
indeterminates.
Thus $\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]\{\Psi\}$ is the ring of differential polynomials in the single
dependent variable $\Psi$, over coefficients involving the three independent
variables $x,y,z$, with $\Delta = \{\delta_x, \delta_y, \delta_z\}$.
$\Psi_x$ is $\delta_x$ applied to~$\Psi$.

In the present work, primes are reserved for a different purpose --- the derivatives
internal to an ODE extension,
where $\Psi'$ means $d\Psi/dv$ for the extension's
own independent variable~$v$.  Such a prime is never a $\Delta$-derivative:
$\Psi'$ is a differential indeterminate in its own right, tied to $\Psi$ by the
chain rule.  See the next subsection for more details.

Constants are differential indeterminates too --- those that every derivation
sends to zero.  We name them $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ and impose $\delta_i c_j = 0$.

\subsection{Describing ODEs}

Ultimately, we are operating in a partial differential ring, but we are
seeking to solve the PDE using ODEs.  How, then, do we describe ODEs using differential polynomials?
Initially, we consider homogenous linear ODEs, but this is not a fundamental limitation;
see below, for example, Figure \ref{nonlinear ansatz figure} and equations \eqref{ansatz 18}.

An $n^{\rm th}$-order homogeneous linear ODE takes the form:

\begin{equation}
\label{analytic ODE}
a_n(v) y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}(v) y^{(n-1)} \cdots a_1(v) y' + a_0(v) y = 0
\end{equation}

where $y^{(n)}, y^{(n-1)}, ... y'$ are derivatives of the dependent variable $y$ with respect to the independent variable $v$.
Note that all of the coefficient functions are univariate functions of $v$.  We will further
restrict them to be polynomials in $v$, but this is also not a fundamental limitation;
see the ans\"atze in Figures \ref{ansatz 17 figure} and \ref{ansatz 13 figure} and equations \eqref{ansatz 17} and \eqref{ansatz 13}.

% How to introduce a new ODE?  First, we wish to avoid the complexities of introducing new derivations,
% so we seek to specify how the ODE behaves under the existing derivations.

To describe equation \eqref{analytic ODE} using differential polynomials, we introduce $n+2$ new indeterminates.
We need $n+1$ indeterminates for $y$ and its first $n$ derivatives, and we
introduce another indeterminate for the {\it independent variable} $v$.

We create a differential polynomial in $y$ and its derivatives
whose coefficients are polynomials in $v$ with constant coefficients (either numeric constants or constant indeterminates).
Thus, the coefficients of the ODE's defining differential polynomial are
selected not from the existing field, but rather from ${\mathbb Q}(v)$, an auxiliary field
constructed using the independent variable $v$.

How do the existing derivations operate on the new indeterminates?
%The independent variable needs
%no special attention; it is the operation of the derivations on the dependent variable and its derivatives that must be specified.
% We can expess our previously existing derivatives in terms of the new one using the chain rule.
All that is required is the chain rule.  We need to specify how the new indeterminates behave under the existing
derivations, and that relationship is analytically:

\begin{equation}
\frac{{\rm d}\Psi}{{\rm d}x} = \frac{{\rm d}\Psi}{{\rm d}v} \frac{{\rm d}v}{{\rm d}x}
\end{equation}

Algebraically, we write:

\begin{equation}
\Psi_x = \Psi' v_x
\end{equation}

We use primes, and not the jet notation subscripts, when writing
the differential polynomial in the ODE case because we are not introducing a new derivation,
nor is the ODE's derivative any of the existing derivatives.

In short, to construct a new ODE extension:

\begin{itemize}
% \item We wish to avoid introducing new derivations.
\item We introduce new indeterminates $\Psi, \Psi',..., \Psi^{(n)}, v$
\item We introduce a new differential polynomial involving only the new indeterminates and constants,
and using no other non-constant indeterminates or derivatives
\item We define the derivatives of $\Psi, \Psi',...,\Psi^{(n-1)}$ using the chain rule
%: \hfil\break $\Psi_x = \Psi' v_x$ \qquad $\Psi'_x = \Psi'' v_x$
%% \item We equate the independent variable $v$ to some parameterized differential polynomial
\end{itemize}

{\bf Example:} In the differential ring $\mathbb{C}[x,y]\{\Psi,\Psi',v\}$, with
$\Delta = \{\delta_x, \delta_y\}$, we can construct an ODE
extension specified by the dependent indeterminate $\Psi$, its derivative $\Psi'$, independent indeterminate $v$, and differential polynomial
$\Psi' = \Psi$.  This differential polynomial
maps to the analytic equation $\frac{d\Psi}{dv}=\Psi$ and is solved
by the analytic functions $Ae^v$ ($A$ is an arbitrary constant).  If $v=x^2+y$, then
the functions $Ae^{x^2+y}$ are solutions to this ODE; the differential polynomials required to model $\Psi=Ae^{x^2+y}$ are:

\begin{equation}
\Psi_x = 2 \Psi' x \qquad
\Psi_y = \Psi' \qquad
\Psi' = \Psi
\end{equation}

which simplify to:

\begin{equation}
\Psi_x = 2 \Psi x \qquad
\Psi_y = \Psi \qquad
\end{equation}

\subsection{Describing Non-Polynomial Functions}

Restricting from differential equations to differential
polynomials is not a severe limitation, as so many elementary and special functions
can be described as the solutions of differential polynomials.  For example, both
$y=\cos \theta$ and $y=\sin \theta$ are solutions to the
analytic equation $y''(\theta) + y(\theta) = 0$ and
can easily be introduced into a system of differential polynomials
using the technique of the previous subsection.

% by adding the differential polynomial equation $y_{\theta\theta} +y=0$ to the system.

For example, consider introducing the function $\cos x$ in the differential ring
$\mathbb{Q}[x,y]\{f,f'\}$, with $\Delta = \{\delta_x, \delta_y\}$.
We can replace $\cos x$ with a new indeterminate, say $f$,
and write $f'' = -f$, where the derivative is taken with respect to $x$.  The technique from the
previous section is used to connect this with the existing derivations, as follows:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
f_x = f' \qquad f_y = 0 \\
f'_x = f'' \qquad f'_y = 0 \\
f'' = -f
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

This can be simplified somewhat by eliminating $f''$:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
f_x = f' \qquad f_y = 0 \\
f'_x = -f \qquad f'_y = 0 \\
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The analytic solutions to these equations will be of the form $A\cos x + B\sin x$, but
this does not present a serious problem.  In fact, it is quite common to find
a space of solutions for a differential equation, and then to impose additional
restrictions (such as boundary conditions) to further restrict those solutions.
% The solutions of $f'' = f$, for example, will be of the form $A\cos x + B\sin x$,
% and imposing the boundary conditions $f(0)=1$ and $f'(0)=0$ would restrict $f$
% to be $\cos x$.
Likewise, we can use $f$ for our calculations with differential polynomials and,
at the conclusion of the calculations,
restrict $f$ to be $\cos x$, not to meet a boundary condition, but to force
$f$ to be the function required by the original problem.

%The best we're likely to reduce to is an equation like $\Psi=\Psi_x$, and it's up to us
%to solve the corresponding analytic equation and conclude that $\Psi$ is a multiple of the exponential of $x$.

Likewise, $g=\exp x$ can be expressed using $g' = g$ and $h=\ln x$ using $h' = x'/x$,
so most elementary and special functions can be expressed using
differential polynomials.

\subsection{Forming an Ansatz}

Our primary goal is to solve a PDE, or a system of them, by finding solutions described using ODEs.
Therefore, all of my current ans\"atze attempt to express solutions to the system using
a finite combination of ODEs.  They do not have to be linear, seperable or homogenous.
They do require degree bounds on all of their constituent polynomials,
in order to obtain a finite number of constant parameters.

Algebraic extensions can also be included, but since
they must be parameterized by a finite number of constants, they can not express
an arbitrary algebraic extension of indefinite degree.

\begin{comment}
To form an ansatz, we construct a tower of ODE and/or algebraic extensions that build from the base differential ring
(used to write our original PDE) and culminates in a newly introduced indeterminate
that is used as the equation's dependent variable.  When the target is a system
of interrelated equations, several parameterized differential polynomials sit at the top of the tower,
each equated to a new indeterminate and used
as one of the system's dependent variables (see Figure \ref{nonlinear ansatz figure});
the algorithm of \S\ref{sec:Solution Algorithm} handles this case with no change
beyond reducing each equation of the system in turn.
\end{comment}

The primary requirement for an ansatz is that it be expressed using differential polynomials.
% as this makes it ammenable to the techniques of differential algebra.
This excludes any kind of boundary conditions (which would require evaluating functions at some boundary)
or function space restrictions (which would require evaluating global integrals of the function),
but admits almost almost any differential equation imaginable.

A good ansatz will be flexible enough to match the solution sought, but require only a small number
of parameters, to make the computation tractable.

We use a graphical notation to describe an ansatz, for a key see Figure \ref{key}.  Blue-purple boxes
denote rings, orange boxes denote ODE extensions, and green boxes denote

denote the ring formed by adjoining the element defined by the orange or green box.

connected by a line to the ring to which they belong.  A black number next to
the line indicates a degree bound on the polynomial, and two numbers
separated by a slash denote a rational function.  For example, 2/2 denotes
a rational function with a second degree numerator and a second degree
denominator.

Two refinements of this notation are used in Figure
\ref{normalized ansatz figure}, where an ansatz has been normalized and its
polynomials are consequently not free.  Where only some of a polynomial's
coefficients remain free, the polynomial is written out in place of the single
white box, with a white box standing for each coefficient that is still free:
$(\coeff + \coeff s)$ is an arbitrary first degree polynomial in $s$, while
$(1 + \coeff s)$ is one whose constant term has been fixed at $1$.  And the
degree bound on a line is written only when every generator of the ring below
may appear to that degree.  A polynomial confined to some of the generators
--- an inner variable built from two of the four coordinates, say --- carries
no number, because no degree bound describes it; the form written out in its
box is the description.

First, the ODE:

\begin{equation}
\label{ansatz 5b}
(a_0 + a_1 v) \Psi'' + (b_0 + b_1 v) \Psi' + (c_0 + c_1 v) \Psi = 0 \\
\end{equation}

Next, the derivatives of $\Psi$ and $\Psi'$, defined using the chain rule:

\begin{equation}
\label{ansatz 5c}
\begin{gathered}
\Psi_x = \Psi' v_x \qquad
\Psi_y = \Psi' v_y \qquad
\Psi_z = \Psi' v_z \\
\Psi'_x = \Psi'' v_x \qquad
\Psi'_y = \Psi'' v_y \qquad
\Psi'_z = \Psi'' v_z \\
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

Finally, the independent variable $v$:

\begin{equation}
\label{ansatz 5a}
v = v_1 x + v_2 y + v_3 z + v_4 r
\end{equation}

Our differential ring is equipped with three derivations $dx$, $dy$, and $dz$.
Our base ring
is $\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]$, and $r$ is a differential indeterminate carrying the
relation $r^2 - (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)$;
from this we select a linear variable $v$ using equation
\eqref{ansatz 5a}.
The $v_i$'s (and the $a_i$'s, $b_i$'s and $c_i$'s) are constants.
We drop the $v_0$ constant term from the
independent variable, because taking
the derivative with respect to $x+3$ is no different from taking
the derivative with respect to $x$.

The element $\Psi$ is expected to solve the PDE, and we use it to write the PDE (not shown here; see section \ref{sec:Example}).

\section{Solution Algorithm}
\label{sec:Solution Algorithm}

Having selected an ansatz, we now
seek to find the constants that allow the ansatz to solve a given system of differential equations, in a sense
made more specific in \S\ref{sec:constant-loci}.

\subsection{Differential Algebra Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

We first recall the notions from differential algebra on which
the hypotheses and theorems below rest.  For the Ritt-reduction apparatus ---
rankings, leaders, initials and separants, reduction ---
a compact description of the relevant concepts can be found in \cite{blop}, \S 3.
For involutivity, simple systems and
the Thomas decomposition we follow \cite{ThomasDecomp}, \S\S\,2--3 (see also \cite{LHRcontrol} \S2), and for the
ideal-theoretic properties of simple systems, \cite{RobertzLNM}, \S\,2.2 (see also \cite{RobertzCIRM}, \S\,3).

A differential ideal $I$ is a subring of a differential ring $R$, closed under arbitrary
derivation and multiplication by arbitrary ring elements.  Given a set of generators $g_1,...,g_n$,
the differential ideal they generate is denoted $[g_1,...,g_n]$.
We write $\sqrt{I} = \{p:\exists n\in Z^+, p^n \in I\}$ for the {\it radical} of a differential ideal $I$, the set of
differential polynomials some power of which lies in~$I$.
We write the {\it saturation} of an differential ideal as $I:S^\infty = \{p: \exists q \in R, qp\in I\}$.

A differential ideal $I$ is {\it consistent} if $1 \notin I$; otherwise it is {\it inconsistent}.

Ritt defined two reduction algorithms: full and partial.
We use Ritt's full reduction algorithm
%% ; see \cite{Ritt} Chapter I, \S 6 or \cite{Kolchin}, Chapter I, \S 9.
(\cite{Ritt}, \S\,I.6; \cite{Kolchin}, \S\,I.9)

%% A comparison of various techniques used to solve systems of polynomial and differential polynomial equations can be found in \cite{aistleitner}.

Ritt's reduction algorithms require a {\it ranking} to be specified on the differential variables.
This is a distinct concept from an ordering on the monomials, as used, for example, in defining a Gr\"obner basis,
and is more in the spirit of triangular decomposition algorithms than Gr\"obner basis algorithms.

A ranking on a differential ring $R$ is an ordering on the ring's differential variables that meets two compatibility conditions:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\label{ranking compatibility}
\forall \delta\in\Theta, \, \forall u,v \in R & \quad u < v \implies \delta u < \delta v & \\
\forall \delta\in\Theta, \, \forall u \in R & \quad u < \delta u
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The standard definition orders only the derivatives of the differential
indeterminates.  We follow \cite{DifferentialAlgebra} in extending a ranking to
the independent variables of the coefficient ring as well, placing them in the
lowest block; nothing in the theory depends on this, but it lets a single
ordering be written for every indeterminate the computation touches.

A ranking is said to be {\it orderly} if meets the additional condition:

\begin{equation}
\forall \delta,\theta \in\Theta,\, \forall u,v \in R \quad {\rm ord}\ \delta < {\rm ord}\ \theta \implies \delta u < \theta v
\end{equation}

Note that an orderly ranking ranks all pure indeterminates lower than any proper derivative.

The {\it leader} of a polynomial is the highest ranking indeterminate or derivative that appears in the polynomial
with a non-zero coefficient.
Writing $u_p$ for the leader of~$p$,
the {\it initial} of $p$ is its leading coefficient regarded as a
univariate polynomial in $u_p$, and the {\it separant} of $p$ is
$s_p = \partial p / \partial u_p$.  For a set $A \subseteq R \setminus K$
we write $I_A$, $S_A$, and $H_A = I_A \cup S_A$ for the sets of initials,
separants, and both.

A differential polynomial $p$ is said to be {\it partially reduced} with respect to a differential polynomial $q$ if
no proper derivative of $q$'s leader appears in $p$, and {\it fully reduced} if it is partially reduced and
$q$'s leader, if it appears in $p$, appears at a lower degree than its degree in $q$.

% To reduce a differential polynomial $p$ by a system $S$ of differential polynomials,
% we see if there exists a polynomial in $S$ any higher derivative of the leader of a polynomial in $S$ appears in $p$.
% If so, we pick the highest ranking

Ritt's full reduction algorithm reduces a differential polynomial $p$ by a system $S$ of differential polynomials
and produces differential polynomials $r$ (the remainder) and $h \in H_S$
such that $r$ is fully reduced with respect to all of the differential polynomials in $S$
and $(hp - r)$ is an element of the differential ideal $[S]$ generated by the elements of $S$ and all their derivatives.
Put another way, $r$ is an element of the saturated differential ideal $[S]:H_S^\infty$.

\smallskip
A set $A \subseteq R \setminus K$ is {\it triangular} if the leaders of its
elements are pairwise distinct, and {\it differentially triangular}
(\cite{blop}, Definition~13) if in addition its elements are pairwise
partially reduced.

\smallskip
The differential Thomas decomposition rests on a constructive completion
condition, phrased through an involutive division.  Fix
an ordering $d_1 < \cdots < d_n$ of the derivations.  Given a finite set $U$ of
derivatives, write each $u \in U$ as $\theta v$ for an indeterminate $v$ and
$\theta = d_1^{\mu_1} \cdots d_n^{\mu_n} \in \Theta$, and call
$\mu(u) = (\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_n)$ its multi-index.  {\it Janet's division}
declares $d_j$ {\it multiplicative} for $u \in U$ if $\mu_j(u)$ is maximal among
the multi-indices of those elements of $U$ that share the indeterminate $v$ and
agree with $u$ in $\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_{j-1}$; the remaining derivations are {\it
non-multiplicative} for~$u$.  A {\it non-multiplicative prolongation} of a
differential polynomial $p$ is $d_j\, p$ for a derivation $d_j$ that is
non-multiplicative for the leader of~$p$.

\smallskip
A differential system $S = (S^=, S^{\ne})$ of equations $S^=$ and inequations
$S^{\ne}$ is {\it involutive} (\cite{ThomasDecomp}, Definition~3.5) if
every non-multiplicative prolongation of an element of $S^=$ reduces to zero
modulo $S^=$.

\smallskip
A differential system $S$,
regarded as a polynomial system in the differential variables that occur in
it, is {\it algebraically simple} (\cite{ThomasDecomp}, Definition~2.2) if

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\rm(A1)] $S$ is {\it triangular}: no two elements of $S$ share a leader,
  and no element of $S$ lies in the base field;
\item[\rm(A2)] $S$ has {\it non-vanishing initials}: for each equation $p$ of
  $S$, with leader $x$, the initial of $p$ is non-zero at every solution of the
  subsystem of $S$ in the variables ranked strictly below~$x$; and
\item[\rm(A3)] $S$ is {\it square-free}: for each equation $p$ of $S$, with
  leader $x$, the univariate polynomial in $x$ obtained by evaluating the
  coefficients of $p$ at a solution of that lower subsystem is square-free, for
  every such solution.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Conditions (A2) and~(A3) are quantified over the solutions of the lower part of
the system rather than imposed at a single point.  (expand on this)
That is what makes them
stable under specialization of lower-ranked variables, and it is the fact on
which Lemma~\ref{lem:specialization} turns.  Condition~(A3) also has a
consequence we use without further comment: a square-free polynomial and its
separant have no common root, so the separants of $S$, like its initials, do not
vanish on the solutions of the lower-ranking subsystems (\cite{ThomasDecomp},
\S\,2, footnote~3).

\smallskip
A system $S = (S^=, S^{\ne})$ is {\it differentially simple}
(\cite{ThomasDecomp}, Definition~3.5) if

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\rm(S1)] $S$ is algebraically simple;
\item[\rm(S2)] $S$ is involutive;
\item[\rm(S3)] $S^=$ is minimal; and
\item[\rm(S4)] no inequation of $S^{\ne}$ is reducible modulo $S^=$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
A decomposition of a system into finitely many differentially simple subsystems
whose solution sets are pairwise disjoint is a {\it differential Thomas
decomposition}.  \cite{ThomasDecomp} gives an algorithm computing one, and
\cite{RobertzLNM} develops the theory at length; \cite{RobertzCIRM} is a shorter and freely available account.

\smallskip
Everything the completeness argument of \S\ref{sec:completeness} requires is
supplied by a single result about simple systems, which we state in the form we
use.  Write $q$ for the product of the initials and separants of the equations
of $S^=$.

\begin{proposition}[\cite{RobertzLNM}, Prop.~2.2.50; \cite{RobertzCIRM}, Prop.~3.31]
\label{prop:simple-membership}
Let $S$ be a simple
differential system with equations $p_1 = 0, \ldots, p_s = 0$, let
$E = [p_1, \ldots, p_s]$ be the differential ideal they generate, and let $q$ be
the product of the initials and separants of $p_1, \ldots, p_s$.  Then
$E : q^\infty$ is a radical differential ideal, and for $p \in R$,
%
\[
\begin{aligned}
p \in E : q^\infty
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
&\text{the pseudo-remainder of $p$ modulo $p_1, \ldots, p_s$} \\
&\text{and their derivatives is zero.}
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proposition}

\smallskip
Proposition \ref{prop:simple-membership} implies that a differentially
simple system is {\it coherent} in the sense of \cite{blop}, Definition~22,
though we do not use this fact.

A companion statement assembles the components of a decomposition.

\begin{proposition}[\cite{RobertzLNM}, Prop.~2.2.72; \cite{RobertzCIRM}, Prop.~3.32]
\label{prop:decomposition-membership}
Let $S$ be a differential system, not assumed simple, with equations
$p_1 = 0, \ldots, p_s = 0$ and inequations $q_1 \ne 0, \ldots, q_t \ne 0$; let
$E = [p_1, \ldots, p_s]$ be the differential ideal generated by the equations
and let $q$ be the product of $q_1, \ldots, q_t$.  Let $S_1, \ldots, S_r$ be a

%
\[
\sqrt{E : q^\infty}
  \;=\; \bigl(E^{(1)} : (q^{(1)})^\infty\bigr)
        \,\cap\,\cdots\,\cap\,
        \bigl(E^{(r)} : (q^{(r)})^\infty\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The Constant Loci}
\label{sec:constant-loci}

We wish to solve a system of differential equations, where the solution must be of a restricted form, parameterized by constants,
the {\it ansatz}.

Given an ansatz $A(c^*) = \{ a_1(c^*),...,a_k(c^*) \}$, a collection of differential polynomials parameterized by constants $c^* \in \mathbb{C}^n$,
and a second collection $\mathcal{P}$ --- the system we wish to solve ---
we now wish to find values for the constants that will solve the system.

The target system is a system in the sense of \S\ref{sec:preliminaries}: it
carries inequations as well as equations.  We write
$\mathcal{P} = (\mathcal{P}^=, \mathcal{P}^{\ne})$ with equations
$\mathcal{P}^= = \{P_1, \ldots, P_t\}$ and inequations
$\mathcal{P}^{\ne} = \{Q_1, \ldots, Q_w\}$, and abbreviate the product of the
inequations as $Q = Q_1 \cdots Q_w$, with $Q = 1$ when $\mathcal{P}^{\ne}$ is
empty.

The inequations are where a problem's non-degeneracy conditions are declared:
that a wave function is not identically zero, that a pressure or a density does
not vanish, that some constant appearing in the ansatz is non-zero.  Without
them the loci defined below are frequently satisfied by a degenerate solution of
no interest --- most often the zero function --- and it is the inequations that
rule those out.

%% It is convenient to define two loci in constant space.

{\bf The Non-degenercy Locus}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nondegeneracy}
N_Q = \{ c^* \in {\mathbb C}^n : 1 \notin [A(c^*)]:Q^\infty \},
\end{equation}

The {\it non-degeneracy locus} $N_Q$ is the set of constants for which the ansatz
alone is consistent in the sense of
\S\ref{sec:preliminaries}, after saturation by the inequations.

{\bf The Consistency Locus}

\begin{equation}
V_\exists = \{ c^* \in {\mathbb C}^n : 1 \notin [A(c^*), \mathcal{P}^=] : Q^\infty \}
\end{equation}

The {\it consistency locus} $V_\exists$ is the set of constants for which the ansatz
{\it together with} the system is consistent.

% The condition
% is on the differential ideal $[A(c^*), \mathcal{P}^=]$ that the two generate
% jointly; the standing assumption that the ansatz {\it by itself} is consistent
% for every $c^*$ is a separate and far weaker statement.

Informally, consistency says that the ansatz and the system have a common
solution respecting the inequations, and we use that language below where it
aids intuition.  Existence of a solution in some function space, however,
is an analytic statement that is not asserted here.

{\bf The Membership Locus}

\begin{equation}
V_{\forall} = \{ c^* \in {\mathbb C}^n : 1 \notin [A(c^*)] : Q^\infty \wedge \mathcal{P}^= \subseteq \sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty} \}
\end{equation}

The {\it membership locus} is the set of constants for which the ansatz is consistent and every equation of
the system lies in the saturated radical differential ideal defined by the
ansatz, meaning that any function satisfying the ansatz {\it and the
inequations} will satisfy every equation of the system.

Taking the radical of the ideal is justified when we will ultimately map the differential indeterminates
into a function space whose range has no nilpotent elements.  Since neither the real nor the complex numbers
have nilpotents, and we are ultimately looking for either real- or complex-valued functions, we
take the radical to simplify both the theory and the implementation.

% \begin{equation}
% V_{\forall} = \{ c^* \in {\mathbb C}^n : P \in [A(c^*)] \}
% \end{equation}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:locus-containment}
The membership locus is a subset of the consistency locus:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:locus-containment}
V_\forall \subseteq V_\exists
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

{\it Proof.}
$\sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty}$ is a differential ideal, and if every element
of $\mathcal{P}^=$ lies in it then it contains both $A(c^*)$ and $\mathcal{P}^=$,
hence contains $[A(c^*), \mathcal{P}^=]$; saturating that containment
by~$Q^\infty$ and using $(I : Q^\infty) : Q^\infty = I : Q^\infty$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal{P}^= \subseteq \sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty} \implies [A(c^*),\mathcal{P}^=] : Q^\infty = \sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty} \\
Q \notin \sqrt{[A(c^*)]} \implies 1 \notin \sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty} \implies 1 \notin \sqrt{[A(c^*), \mathcal{P}^=] : Q^\infty} \\
V_\forall \subseteq V_\exists
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
%
\hfill $\square$

\begin{example}
\label{ex:three-loci}
In the differential ring $\mathbb{C}[t]\{\Psi, c\}$, with the single derivation
$\delta_t$ and $c$ constant, consider the ansatz
\begin{equation}
A(c) = \{ \Psi_{tt} - c \Psi , c_t\}
\end{equation}
and the system $\mathcal{P}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}^= = \{ \Psi_t - \Psi \} \qquad \mathcal{P}^{\ne} = \emptyset
\end{equation}

$\{A(c) \cup P\}$ is not differentially simple,
but splits into two differentially simple subsystems:
\begin{equation}
\label{ex:three-loci two systems}
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal{S}_1^= = \{ \Psi, c_t \} \qquad \mathcal{S}_1^{\ne} = \emptyset \\
\mathcal{S}_2^= = \{ \Psi_t - \Psi,  c - 1 \} \qquad \mathcal{S}_2^{\ne} = \{ \Psi \}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
$\mathcal{S}_1$, in particular, has no restriction on $c$ (other than it be constant)
and no terms in $\mathbb{C}$, so $1 \notin [\mathcal{S}_1^=]$ and $V_\exists = \mathbb{C}$.

To compute $V_\forall$, we need to identify the constants where
$\mathcal{P} \subseteq \sqrt{[A(c)]}$.  $A(c)$ is a simple differential
system, and $\Psi_t-\Psi$ remains unchanged after pseudo-reduction by $A(c)$,
so by Proposition \ref{prop:simple-membership}, $\Psi_t-\Psi$ is not in $[A(c)]$
for any value of $c$, and $V_\forall$ is empty.

\begin{equation*}
V_\exists  = \mathbb{C} \qquad
V_\forall = \emptyset
\end{equation*}
\qed
\end{example}

\begin{example}
\label{ex:three-loci-2}
In example \ref{ex:three-loci-2}, we saw how the zero function dominates
the constant loci when homogenous equations are used.
Continuing the example, we now address this problem by using an inequation
to strip out the zero solution.  The ansatz remains the same:

\begin{equation}
A(c) = \{ \Psi_{tt} - c \Psi \}
\end{equation}
We add an inequality to the system $\mathcal{P}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}^= = \{ \Psi_t - \Psi \} \qquad \mathcal{P}^{\ne} = \{\Psi\}
\end{equation}

The inequality kills the first system in \eqref{ex:three-loci two systems}, so we are left with only $\mathcal{S}_2$:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal{S}_2^= = \{ \Psi_t - \Psi,  c - 1 \} \qquad \mathcal{S}_2^{\ne} = \{ \Psi \}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

Only $c=1$ solves this system, so $V_\exists = \{1\}$, and $V_\forall$ remains vacuous:

\begin{equation*}
V_\exists  = \{ 1 \} \qquad
V_\forall = \emptyset
\end{equation*}
\qed
\end{example}

It is perhaps easiest to understand these examples analytically.
For any constant $c$, $\Psi=0$ is a solution to both the ansatz and the PDE, so without the inequation $V_\exists = \mathbb{C}$.
If $c=1$, then $ae^t$ is a solution to both $A(1)$ and $P$, and 1 is the only value of $c$ for which they have a common non-zero solution,
so imposing $\Psi \ne 0$ cuts the consistency locus down to $\{ 1 \}$.
However, although both $ae^t$ and $ae^{-t}$ solve $A(1)$, only $ae^t$ solves $P$,
so a solution to the ansatz does not necessarily solve the PDE and $V_\forall = \emptyset$.
The containment \eqref{eq:locus-containment} reads
$\emptyset \subseteq \{1\}$, as it must.

\subsection{Computing the Consistency Locus}
\label{sec:consistency-locus}

We now present algorithms to compute the consistency and membership loci.

The most straightforward way to compute the consistency locus $V_\exists$ is to proceed
as its definition suggests: combine the system and the ansatz together as the generators of a singular differential
ideal.  We do not wish to treat the constants as indeterminates (as they would always be non-zero), but rather
as parameters, and seek conditions on those parameters that would yield a non-trivial
differential ideal where $1 \notin [A(c^*), \mathcal{P}^=] : Q^\infty$.  This suggests the
use of a parametric algorithm, such as parametric Rosenfeld-Gr\"obner \cite{Fakouri2018} or a
differential Thomas decomposition \cite{ThomasDecomp, RobertzLNM}.  We have adopted
differential Thomas decomposition primarily because it has convenient theoretical
properties that allow us to prove completeness of the algorithm.

We record this direct approach below, as Algorithm~\ref{alg:consistency}, both
for completeness and because its simplicity makes a good introduction to the
rest of this section.

\begin{comment}
The consistency locus admits a much simpler algorithm than the membership
locus, because its definition lets us decompose the ansatz and the target
system's equations {\it together}, rather than decomposing the ansatz alone
and testing the target system against each resulting component afterward.
However, the {\tt DifferentialDecompose} step is more computationally
demanding, precisely because its input involves more polynomials.
\end{comment}

\begin{definition}[\cite{CTD}, Definition 6]
A {\it constructible subset} of $\mathbb{C}^n$ is any finite union
\[
(A_1 \setminus B_1 ) \cup \ldots \cup (A_e \setminus B_e )
\]
where $A_1, \ldots , A_e , B_1 , \ldots , B_e$ are algebraic varieties in $\mathbb{C}^n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
The {\it cell} of a differentially simple system $(S^=, S^{\ne})$,
computed with the constant parameters $c = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$ ranked below all other indeterminates,
is the constant locus in $\mathbb{C}^n$ that solves the equations and inequations of $(S^=, S^{\ne})$
that lie in $\mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$.
\end{definition}

\medskip
\noindent
By \cite{ThomasDecomp}, Remark~2.3, every solution of the
subsystem in the strictly lower-ranked variables extends to a solution of the
next level of a simple system, and conversely every solution restricts; so,
inductively, the projection of $\mathrm{Sol}(S^=, S^{\ne})$ onto $\mathbb{C}^n$
is exactly the solution set of the constant-only part of $(S^=, S^{\ne})$.
Deciding whether $c^*$ lies in the cell is therefore a matter of substituting
$c^*$ into finitely many polynomials in the constants and checking that the
equations vanish and the inequations do not.
The cell is thus quasi-affine ---
cut out by equations {\it and} inequations --- and is represented as a constructible set.

The constant-only equations and inequations need only be a subset of $(S^=, S^{\ne})$,
so cells belonging to different
components may overlap, even though the components themselves are disjoint,
since the case distinction may be made at the level of non-constant indeterminates.

We write ideals of $\mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$ in fraktur ($\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}$)
and $\langle F \rangle$ for the ideal generated by a finite
set $F \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$; $(0)$ and $(1)$ are the zero
and unit ideals.

A pair $(\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b})$ of ideals represents the constructible
set
%
\[
\mathrm{Z}(\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}) \;:=\; V(\mathfrak{a}) \setminus V(\mathfrak{b}) \;\subseteq\; \mathbb{C}^n,
\]
%
and a finite set $\mathcal{W}$ of such pairs represents $\mathrm{Z}(\mathcal{W})
:= \bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{W}} \mathrm{Z}(B)$.  Following \cite{CTD}, \S2, we
reserve $\mathrm{Z}(\cdot)$ for this passage from a representation to the set
it represents, so that no symbol stands for both at once.  A product of
ideals vanishes exactly on the union of their varieties,
$V(\mathfrak{a}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{a}_m) = \bigcup_k V(\mathfrak{a}_k)$; a
generating set for the product is $\{\, g_1 \cdots g_m : g_k \text{ a
generator of } \mathfrak{a}_k \,\}$, which for principal factors recovers the
ordinary product of polynomials, e.g.\ $h_i$ below.  The empty product is
$(1)$, so $\mathfrak{a}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{a}_m = (1)$ when $m = 0$, and
$V((1)) = \emptyset$.

\begin{algorithm}[ConsistencyLocus]
\label{alg:consistency}
\mbox{}

{\bf Input:} A differential polynomial ring
$R = \mathbb{Q}[x_1, \ldots, x_m]\{u_1, \ldots, u_k, c_1, \ldots, c_n\}$ with
derivations $\Delta = \{\delta_1, \ldots, \delta_m\}$ satisfying
$\delta_i x_j = \delta_{ij}$ and $\delta_i c_l = 0$; the target system
$\mathcal{P} = (\mathcal{P}^=, \mathcal{P}^{\ne})$, with equations
$\mathcal{P}^= = \{P_1, \ldots, P_t\} \subset R$ and inequations
$\mathcal{P}^{\ne} = \{Q_1, \ldots, Q_w\} \subset R$, abbreviated $Q = Q_1
\cdots Q_w$ as in \S\ref{sec:constant-loci}; an
ansatz $A(c) \subset R$, a finite set of differential polynomials
parameterized by the constants $c = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$; and a block ranking
$\prec$ with
%
\[
\{u_1, \ldots, u_k\} \;\gg\; \{c_1, \ldots, c_n\} \;\gg\; \{x_1, \ldots, x_m\}.
\]

{\bf Output:} A constructible subset of $\mathbb{C}^n$, presented as a finite
set $\mathcal{W}$ of pairs $(\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b})$ of ideals,
and so as a finite union of locally
closed sets: $\mathrm{Z}(\mathcal{W}) = V_\exists$, the consistency locus
$V_\exists = \{\, c^* \in \mathbb{C}^n : 1 \notin [A(c^*), \mathcal{P}^=] : Q^\infty \,\}$.

{\bf Algorithm:}
\begin{pseudocode}
\item $(S_1, \ldots, S_s) \leftarrow \mathrm{DifferentialDecompose}\bigl( (A(c) \cup \mathcal{P}^=, \{Q\}), \, \prec \bigr)$
\item {\bf for} $i = 1, \ldots, s$ {\bf do}
\item \algind $E_i \leftarrow S^=_i \cap \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$
\item \algind $h_i \leftarrow \prod \bigl( S^{\ne}_i \cap \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n] \bigr)$
\item \algind $W_i \leftarrow \bigl\{\, (\langle E_i \rangle, \langle h_i \rangle) \,\bigr\}$
\item {\bf end for}
\item {\bf return} $\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{s} W_i$
\end{pseudocode}
\end{algorithm}

Each component $S_i$ of the decomposition is, by construction, a
differentially simple specialization of $A(c) \cup \mathcal{P}^=$ together
with the target's inequations $Q$.
%By the cell argument used in \S\ref{sec:completeness} (\cite{ThomasDecomp}, Remark~2.3),
Every $c^*$ in
$S_i$'s cell $C_i = V(E_i) \setminus V(h_i)$ extends to a genuine solution of
the whole specialized system,
%, respecting $Q \ne 0$ along with everything else in $S_i^{\ne}$;
so $c^* \in V_\exists$ exactly when it lies in the cell
of {\it some} component, and $V_\exists = \bigcup_{i=1}^{s} C_i$.

For simplicity of the presentation, no attempt is made to take radicals of
the ideals, factor them into prime ideals, or detect duplication between them.
An actual implementation of these algorithms would likely do all of this.

\vfill\eject

\subsection{Computing the Membership Locus}
\label{sec:membership-locus}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:core} computes the membership locus.  It follows the same
overall shape as Algorithm~\ref{alg:consistency} but decomposes only the ansatz, and
separately tests the target system against each resulting component.
It tends to be less computationally demanding that Algorithm~\ref{alg:consistency}.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:core} has two branches which compute two different loci: the membership
locus $V_\forall$ and an intermediate locus $V_{\exists\forall}$ which lies
between the membership locus and the consistency locus.  The distinction
between them lies in how they handle constants which appear in multiple
subsystems.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
locus & subsystems & solutions & all of $\mathcal{P}^=$ vanishes \ldots \\
\hline
$V_\forall$          & $\forall$ & $\forall$ & on every solution \\
$V_{\exists\forall}$ & $\exists$ & $\forall$ & on some subsystem \\
$V_\exists$          & $\exists$ & $\exists$ & at one solution \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

It is possible for multiple subsystems to lie over a single constant.
The membership locus proper requires $\mathcal{P}^=$ to belong to every such subsystem.
The intermediate locus, on the other hand, requires only membership in a single subsystem.
The intermediate locus is somewhat easier to compute
and may be preferred when our primary interest is in the consistency locus.
In that case, there is little justification for the extra work required
to discard solutions outside the membership locus; the relationship
between the three loci is as follows:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intermediate-sandwich}
V_\forall \;\subseteq\; V_{\exists\forall} \;\subseteq\; V_\exists .
\end{equation}

Writing
$\mathrm{Z}(C_i)$ for the {\it cell} of the
component, and $\mathrm{Z}(W_i)$ for the solution variety,
the two loci
are constructed as follows:
%
\[
V_\forall \;=\; \bigcap_{i=1}^{s} \bigl( \mathrm{Z}(W_i) \cup (\mathbb{C}^n \setminus \mathrm{Z}(C_i)) \bigr)
\qquad
V_{\exists\forall} \;=\; \bigcup_{i=1}^{s} \mathrm{Z}(W_i)
\]

\vfill\eject

\begin{algorithm}[MembershipLocus]
\label{alg:core}
\mbox{}

{\bf Input:} A differential polynomial ring
$R = \mathbb{Q}[x_1, \ldots, x_m]\{u_1, \ldots, u_k, c_1, \ldots, c_n\}$ with
derivations $\Delta = \{\delta_1, \ldots, \delta_m\}$ satisfying
$\delta_i x_j = \delta_{ij}$ and $\delta_i c_l = 0$; the target system
$\mathcal{P} = (\mathcal{P}^=, \mathcal{P}^{\ne})$, with equations
$\mathcal{P}^= = \{P_1, \ldots, P_t\} \subset R$ and inequations
$\mathcal{P}^{\ne} = \{Q_1, \ldots, Q_w\} \subset R$, abbreviated $Q = Q_1
\cdots Q_w$ as in \S\ref{sec:constant-loci}; an
ansatz $A(c) \subset R$, a finite set of differential polynomials
parameterized by the constants $c = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$; a block ranking $\prec$
with
%
\[
\{u_1, \ldots, u_k\} \;\gg\; \{c_1, \ldots, c_n\} \;\gg\; \{x_1, \ldots, x_m\};
\]
%
and a flag $\mu \in \{\forall, \, \exists\forall\}$ selecting the assembly.

{\bf Output:} A constructible subset of $\mathbb{C}^n$, presented as a finite
set $\mathcal{W}$ of pairs $(\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b})$ of ideals 
so that $\mathrm{Z}(\mathcal{W})$ is a finite union
of locally closed sets.  For $\mu = \forall$ it is the
membership locus $V_\forall$.  For
$\mu = \exists\forall$ it is the intermediate locus $V_{\exists\forall}$, which
satisfies $V_\forall \subseteq V_{\exists\forall} \subseteq V_\exists$ whenever
the ansatz is consistent throughout $\mathbb{C}^n$ --- a condition the algorithm
reports, as described below.

{\bf Algorithm:}
\begin{pseudocode}
\item $(S_1, \ldots, S_s) \leftarrow \mathrm{DifferentialDecompose}\bigl( (A(c), \{Q\}), \, \prec \bigr)$
\item {\bf for} $i = 1, \ldots, s$ {\bf do}
\item \algind $J_i \leftarrow \emptyset$
\item \algind {\bf for} $j = 1, \ldots, t$ {\bf do}
\item \algind\algind $r \leftarrow \mathrm{FullReduce}(P_j, S^=_i)$
\item \algind\algind $J_i \leftarrow J_i \cup \mathrm{Coeffs}\bigl(r, \, \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]\bigr)$
\item \algind {\bf end for}
\item \algind $E_i \leftarrow S^=_i \cap \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$
\item \algind $h_i \leftarrow \prod \bigl( S^{\ne}_i \cap \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n] \bigr)$
\item \algind $C_i \leftarrow (\langle E_i \rangle, \langle h_i \rangle)$
\item \algind $\mathfrak{h}_i \leftarrow \prod_{q \in S^{\ne}_i} \langle \mathrm{Coeffs}(q, \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]) \rangle$
\item \algind $W_i \leftarrow \bigl\{\, (\langle J_i \cup E_i \rangle, \mathfrak{h}_i) \bigr\}$
\item {\bf end for}
\item {\bf if} $\mu = \forall$ {\bf then}
\item \algind $\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathrm{RefiningPartition}\bigl( \{((0), (1)), W_1, C_1, \ldots, W_s, C_s\} \bigr)$
\item \algind $D \leftarrow \bigl\{\, B \;:\; (B, \iota) \in \mathcal{B}, \; C_i \in \iota \implies W_i \in \iota \text{ for every } i \,\bigr\}$
\item \algind {\bf return} $\bigl\{\, (\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{b}) \;:\; (\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}) \in D, \; \mathfrak{p} \in \mathrm{minAss}(\mathfrak{a} : \mathfrak{b}^\infty) \,\bigr\}$
\item {\bf else}
\item \algind {\bf return} $\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{s} W_i$
\item {\bf end if}
\end{pseudocode}
\end{algorithm}

We now summarize and explain the algorithm.

First, we compute a differential Thomas decomposition of the system
$(A(c), \{Q\})$ --- the ansatz together with the target's inequations,
carried along as the single inequation $Q$ --- which produces a partition of
the jet space into quasi-affine sets (i.e, defined by both equations and
inequations), each equiped with a specialization of the ansatz in a
differentially simple form.  Feeding $Q$ into the decomposition itself, rather
than checking it afterward, lets $Q$ participate in the case-splitting: each
component's inequations $S^{\ne}_i$ then already carry whatever of $Q$'s
non-degeneracy survives on that component.
For each differentially simple subsystem in the decomposition,
we use Ritt's full reduction algorithm to reduce each $P_j$
modulo the subsystem, obtaining remainders $r_1, \ldots, r_t$, and project them
onto the space of the $n$ constant parameters
$c = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$.
The subsystem's own inequations $S^{\ne}_i$ --- which, by construction, now
carry $Q$'s contribution along with the rest --- need no further reduction:
condition (S4) of differential simplicity already guarantees that no
inequation of $S^{\ne}_i$ is reducible modulo $S^=_i$, so
$\mathrm{Coeffs}(q, \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n])$ can be read off each
$q \in S^{\ne}_i$ directly.  That coefficient set need not generate a prime
ideal, so we decompose it too: $K_i$ collects, over every $q \in S^{\ne}_i$,
the minimal associated primes of $\langle \mathrm{Coeffs}(q, \mathbb{Q}[c_1,
\ldots, c_n]) \rangle$, each $\mathfrak{q} \in K_i$ an irreducible piece of
some inequation's identically-zero locus.  A prime $\mathfrak{p}$ from
$\mathrm{minAss}$ is discarded outright when some $\mathfrak{q} \in K_i$ is
entirely contained in it, since then $V(\mathfrak{p}) \subseteq
V(\mathfrak{q})$ and every point of $V(\mathfrak{p})$ is degenerate; a
surviving $\mathfrak{p}$ is instead paired with $\mathfrak{h}_i =
\prod_{\mathfrak{q} \in K_i} \mathfrak{q}$, so that $\mathrm{Z}(\mathfrak{p},
\mathfrak{h}_i)$ denotes exactly $V(\mathfrak{p}) \setminus
\bigcup_{\mathfrak{q} \in K_i} V(\mathfrak{q})$ --- excluding precisely the
points where some inequation degenerates, not merely discarding whichever
components are degenerate {\it everywhere}.  This subsumes $h_i$: an
already-constant inequation of $S^{\ne}_i$ has coefficient set $\{q\}$, whose
minimal primes are the $\langle q_k \rangle$ for the distinct irreducible
factors $q_k$ of $q$; since $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime, some $\langle q_k
\rangle \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ exactly when $q \in \mathfrak{p}$, the test
$h_i$ was already built from.

Membership of $P_j$ in $\sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty}$ is a statement about $P_j$ as a
polynomial, not about its value at any particular point.
Accordingly, we split each term of each reduced equation $r_j$ into a power product
in the non-constant indeterminates and a coefficient in the constants alone,
gathering the terms that share a power product:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:remainder-expansion}
r_j \;=\; \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_j} m_{j\alpha} \, p_{j\alpha}(c),
\qquad j = 1, \ldots, t,
\end{equation}
%
where the $m_{j\alpha}$ are the $N_j$ distinct power products occurring in $r_j$
and each coefficient $p_{j\alpha}(c)$ is a polynomial in the constants.

It will be convenient to name the set of coefficients the expansion
produces, and to carry in the notation the ring they are taken from.
Let $A \subseteq R$ be a subring over which $R$ is a polynomial ring in the
remaining indeterminates, so that each $r \in R$ has a unique expansion
$r = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N} m_\alpha \, p_\alpha$ with the $m_\alpha$ distinct
power products in those indeterminates and $p_\alpha \in A$; put
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coeff-operator}
\mathrm{Coeffs}(r, A) \;=\; \{\, p_1, \ldots, p_N \,\} \;\subset\; A .
\end{equation}
%
The only case we need is $A = \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$, for which the
remaining indeterminates are exactly the non-constant ones and
$\mathrm{Coeffs}(r_j, \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n])$ is the coefficient set
$\{p_{j1}, \ldots, p_{jN_j}\}$ of \eqref{eq:remainder-expansion}.

Distinct monomials in a polynomial ring are linearly independent over
$\mathbb{C}$, so $r_j$ vanishes identically at $c^*$ if and only if every one of
its coefficients does.  The projection step therefore replaces the reduced
system $r_1, \ldots, r_t$ by the single system
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:projection-system}
\begin{gathered}
p(c) = 0 \quad \text{for every } p \in J, \\[2pt]
J \;=\; \bigcup_{j=1}^{t} \mathrm{Coeffs}\bigl(r_j, \; \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]\bigr)
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The system $J \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$ defines an algebraic variety
%
\[
\mathcal{V} \;=\; V(J) \;\subseteq\; \mathbb{C}^n,
\]
%
which need not be irreducible.  Because only the zero locus matters, the
decomposition we want is into irreducible components, and these are given by the
minimal associated primes: under the well-known correspondence between prime
ideals and irreducible varieties (\cite{iva}, Corollary~4.5.4), the minimal
primes of the ideal generated by $J$ cut out the irreducible components
of~$\mathcal{V}$, and several algorithms have been proposed and implemented to
compute them (\cite{GTZ}, among others).

Each subsystem's inequations can include inequations in $\mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$,
which must be preserved with the minimal primes, using the ideal notation
introduced in \S\ref{sec:consistency-locus}.

The algorithm calls four subroutines, each taken from the
literature and used unmodified; the work is in what is handed to them and how
their results are combined.  We name them as their sources do, and give the
source's own numbering where the source numbers its algorithms.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lXl@{}}
\hline
subroutine & what it computes & source \\
\hline
$\mathrm{DifferentialDecompose}$
  & a differential Thomas decomposition: a partition of the solution set of a
    differential system into finitely many differentially simple subsystems
  & \cite{ThomasDecomp}, Alg.~3.6 \\[3pt]
$\mathrm{FullReduce}$
  & Ritt's full reduction: the remainder $r$, fully reduced with respect to the

  & \cite{Ritt}, \S6 \\[3pt]
$\mathrm{minAss}$
  & for an ideal $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]$, the

  & \cite{GTZ}, Cor.~3.2(v), \S9 \\[3pt]
$\mathrm{RefiningPartition}$
  & for a finite family of constructible sets, a finite set of pairs
    $(B, \iota)$ in which the $B$ are pairwise disjoint, $\iota$ is the

  & \cite{CSTools}, \S3 \\[3pt]

\subsection{Completeness of the Algorithm}
\label{sec:completeness}

Two questions arise.  If some constant lies in $V_\forall$, the membership
locus --- will the algorithm always find it?  And conversely, is everything the
algorithm returns genuinely in $V_\forall$?
Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness} answers both at once for a single component of
the decomposition, and Corollary~\ref{cor:assembly} assembles the components.

\medskip
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:specialization}
Let $(S^=, S^{\ne})$ be a differentially simple system,
computed with the constant
parameters $c = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$ ranked below all other indeterminates.
If $c^* \in \mathbb{C}^n$ lies in the system's cell, then the specialized system $(S^=(c^*), S^{\ne}(c^*))$
is again differentially simple.
\end{lemma}

{\it Proof.}
Because the constants are ranked below every other indeterminate and conditions
(A2) and (A3) of algebraic simplicity are quantified over solution sets that
contain the whole cell, they are preserved over constants that solve the system.
Since the initial of each equation is non-zero, the leaders do not change
under specialization, and (A1) is preserved as well.  Therefore (S1) persists.

(A2) ensures that initials are non-zero at any $c^*$ in the cell,
so the leaders of the system's equations remain unchanged.
Janet's division's multiplicative/non-multiplicative split is computed from
the leaders alone, so this also remains unchanged.  The reductions
required by (S2) remain unchanged, and if they are zero for generic
constants $c$, they are zero for any specialization $c^*$.
The minimality required by (S3) is computed based on leaders
and reductive derivations, which remain unchanged,
and the reductions prohibited by (S4) are also based on
leaders and remain unchanged.
\hfill $\square$

\medskip
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:completeness}
Let $(S^=, S^{\ne})$ be a differentially simple system,
computed with the constant
parameters $c = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)$ ranked below all other indeterminates.
Write $S$ for its equations $S^=$ --- the inequations $S^{\ne}$ play no
further role in this theorem --- let $H_S = I_S \cup S_S$ denote the set of
initials and separants of~$S$, and let $P$ be a differential polynomial in
the same ring.

Apply Ritt's full reduction to $P$ modulo $S$, obtaining a
remainder $r$ and a power product $h$ of elements of $H_S$ such that
%
\begin{equation}
\label{ritt reduction 1}
h \cdot P - r \in [S]
\end{equation}
%
with $r$ fully reduced with respect to $S$.
Write $r = \sum_\alpha m_{\alpha} \cdot p_{\alpha}(c)$, where
the $m_{\alpha}$
are distinct power products in the non-constant indeterminates and the
$p_{\alpha}$ are polynomials in the constants, so that
$\mathrm{Coeffs}\bigl(r, \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]\bigr) = \{p_{1}, \ldots, p_{N}\}$.  Let
%
\[
\mathcal{V} \;=\; V\Bigl(
   \mathrm{Coeffs}\bigl(r, \, \mathbb{Q}[c_1, \ldots, c_n]\bigr) \Bigr)
   \;\subseteq\; \mathbb{C}^n
\]
%
be the variety
defined by setting all of these constant coefficients to zero.

Let $q$ be the product of the initials and separants of $S$, and suppose $c^*$
lies in the cell of the system.  Then
%
\[
c^* \in \mathcal{V}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P \in [S(c^*)] : q(c^*)^\infty
\]

\end{theorem}

{\it Proof.}
Ritt's full reduction algorithm produces $h$ and $r$ satisfying \eqref{ritt reduction 1}, with
$r$ fully reduced with respect to~$S$.
Evaluating the constants at $c = c^*$ gives
%
\[
h(c^*) \cdot P - r(c^*) \in [S(c^*)] \subseteq [S(c^*)]:q(c^*)^\infty
\]
%
Since $c^*$ lies in the cell, Lemma~\ref{lem:specialization} ensures that
$(S^=(c^*), S^{\ne}(c^*))$ is a simple differential system, and this
implies that $h(c^*)$ is not zero, since initials and separants (maybe a lemma for this)
are not zero on simple differential systems.  Therefore
%
\[
r(c^*) \in [S(c^*)]:q(c^*)^\infty
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P \in [S(c^*)]:q(c^*)^\infty
\]
%

Since $r$ is fully reduced w.r.t. $S(c^*)$, Proposition~\ref{prop:simple-membership} gives
%
\[
r(c^*) = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
r(c^*) \in [S(c^*)] : q(c^*)^\infty .
\]
%

We get the following equivalence:
%
\[
P \in [S(c^*)]:q(c^*)^\infty
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
r(c^*) = 0
\]

Now the power products $m_{\alpha}$ are distinct monomials in a polynomial
ring, hence linearly independent over~$\mathbb{C}$, so $r(c^*) = 0$ if and only
if every coefficient $p_{\alpha}(c^*) = 0$.  The result follows.

\hfill $\square$

\medskip
Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness} shows how a variety in constant space is
associated with membership of a single polynomial in a saturated differential
ideal.  A differentially simple system, however, imposes membership
conditions on several polynomials at once --- an equation for each
polynomial that must lie in the saturated ideal, and, in general, an
inequation for each polynomial that must not --- and the following
corollary assembles the per-polynomial varieties of
Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness} into a single constructible set for the whole
system.

\medskip
\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:system-locus}
Let $(S^=, S^{\ne})$ be a differentially simple system as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}, and let $\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{N} \subset R$ be
finite sets of differential polynomials in the same ring --- equations that
must lie in the saturated ideal $[S(c^*)] : q(c^*)^\infty$, and inequations
that must not, respectively.  For each $P \in \mathcal{E} \cup \mathcal{N}$
let $\mathcal{V}_P \subseteq \mathbb{C}^n$ be the variety produced by
Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}, and put
%
\[
\mathcal{V} \;=\;
   \Bigl(\, \bigcap_{P \in \mathcal{E}} \mathcal{V}_P \,\Bigr)
   \;\setminus\;
   \Bigl(\, \bigcup_{P \in \mathcal{N}} \mathcal{V}_P \,\Bigr).
\]
%
Then $\mathcal{V}$ is a constructible set, and for every $c^*$ in the cell of
$(S^=, S^{\ne})$,
%
\[
c^* \in \mathcal{V}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathcal{E} \subset [S(c^*)] : q(c^*)^\infty
\ \text{ and }\
\mathcal{N} \cap \bigl( [S(c^*)] : q(c^*)^\infty \bigr) = \emptyset .
\]
\end{corollary}

{\it Proof.}
The set $A = \bigcap_{P \in \mathcal{E}} \mathcal{V}_P$ is a finite
intersection of algebraic varieties and so is itself a variety, as is
$B = \bigcup_{P \in \mathcal{N}} \mathcal{V}_P$; thus $\mathcal{V} = A
\setminus B$ is constructible, being of the form required by the definition
of a constructible subset.

Fix $c^*$ in the cell.  Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}, applied to each
$P \in \mathcal{E} \cup \mathcal{N}$ in turn against the same system $S$ ---
and hence the same $q$ --- gives $c^* \in \mathcal{V}_P \iff P \in [S(c^*)] :
q(c^*)^\infty$.  So $c^* \in A$ exactly when every equation of $\mathcal{E}$
lies in the saturated ideal, and $c^* \notin B$ exactly when no inequation
of $\mathcal{N}$ does; conjoining the two gives the claimed equivalence.
\hfill $\square$

\medskip
The membership
locus of the ansatz as a whole is recovered by intersecting over the
decomposition.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:assembly}
Let $\mathcal{V}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{V}_r$ be the constructible sets produced by
Corollary~\ref{cor:system-locus}, taking $\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{P}^=$ and
$\mathcal{N} = \emptyset$, from the components $S_1, \ldots, S_r$ of a
differential Thomas decomposition of the ansatz.  Then, for every
$c^* \in \mathbb{C}^n$,
%
\[
c^* \in V_\forall
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
c^* \in \mathcal{V}_i \ \text{ for every $i$ whose cell contains $c^*$}.
\]
\end{corollary}

{\it Proof.}
By Proposition \ref{prop:decomposition-membership},
%
\[
\sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty}
  \;=\; \bigl(E^{(1)} : (q^{(1)})^\infty\bigr)
        \,\cap\,\cdots\,\cap\,
        \bigl(E^{(r)} : (q^{(r)})^\infty\bigr).
\]

For a given $c^*$, components of this intersection whose cell does not include $c^*$
simplify to $R$, either because a non-zero constant lies in $E^{(i)}$ or because
$q^{(i)}$ is zero.  The result follows directly.

If no cell contains $c^*$ then
the intersection above equals the whole
ring, so $\sqrt{[A(c^*)] : Q^\infty} = (1)$
and the ansatz is inconsistent at this value of $c^*$.
\hfill $\square$

The per-component test decides membership in the {\it saturated} ideal of
that component, while intersecting over the decomposition decides membership in
the {\it radical} differential ideal of the ansatz --- which is the membership
locus as defined in \S\ref{sec:constant-loci}.

\begin{equation}
\label{schrodinger hydrogen 2}
-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 \Psi - \frac{1}{r}\Psi = E \Psi
\end{equation}

where $\nabla$ is the Laplacian, $\Psi$ is a complex-valued wavefunction defined over real 3-dimensional space,
and $r$ is the distance to the origin.  We choose to work in Cartesian coordinates, so $r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2$,
and we use Hartree atomic units to render the equation dimensionless.
This is the case $t = 1$ of \S\ref{sec:Solution Algorithm}: the system
$\mathcal{P}^=$ has the single element obtained by clearing the denominator in
\eqref{schrodinger hydrogen 2}, and we write $P$ for it throughout this section.
The Navier--Stokes example of \S\ref{sec:NavierStokes} exercises the general
case.

We work in the following differential ring:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hydrogen-ring}
R \;=\; \mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]\{\,\Psi, \Psi', \Psi'', v, r,\;
   E, a_0, a_1, b_0, b_1, c_0, c_1, v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4 \,\}
\end{equation}

\noindent
with $\Delta = \{\delta_x, \delta_y, \delta_z\}$.  To elaborate,
\begin{itemize}
   \item the independent variables $x, y, z$ lie in the coefficient ring, where
   $\delta_i x_j = \delta_{ij}$;
   \item $E, a_0, \ldots, v_4$ are the eleven constants, sent to zero by every
   derivation.  $E$ is a constant of the PDE rather than of the ansatz, but the
   algorithm makes no distinction: all eleven are ranked together in the lowest
   block, and the membership locus is a subset of $\mathbb{C}^{11}$ having $E$
   among its coordinates.  This is what allows the eigenvalue to be solved for
   rather than supplied;
   \item $\Psi, \Psi', \Psi'', v, r$ are the five non-constant differential
   indeterminates.  They will ultimately be mapped into a function space, so it
   is often convenient to think of them as functions; algebraically, all that is
   asserted of them here is that they are not constants.  Note that $\Psi'$ and
   $\Psi''$ are separate indeterminates, not $\Delta$-derivatives of $\Psi$ ---
   they are tied to $\Psi$ by the chain-rule equations of the ansatz, and $r$
   likewise carries $r^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2$ in the system rather than as a
   quotient of the ring;
   \item the ranking is specified below.
\end{itemize}

As an example, let's analyze ideal \eqref{ideal:1}.
$a_0=0$, and we can always multiply our homogenous differential polynomial by a constant without affecting our result, so we can set $a_1=1$.
Likewise, we can multiply our variable $v$ by a constant and that will only change our coefficients by constants,
and $v_1=v_2=v_3=0$, so we can normalize by setting $v_4=1$.  This immediately implies that $b_0 = c_0 = 2$ and simplifies ideal \eqref{ideal:2} to:

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

v=r \\

\end{equation}

Ideal \eqref{ideal:2} is the most unusual of the four.
$a_0=0$ and we can set $a_1=1$, by the same logic as above.
If we limit our $v_i$ coefficients to be real, all of their squares must

\begin{equation}
\left(v_{1}^{2} + v_{2}^{2} + v_{3}^{2} - 1, v_{4} - 1, c_{0} - 1, c_{1}, b_{1}, 1 - b_{0}, a_1 - 1, a_{0}, E\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
E = 0 \\
a_0 = 0 \qquad
a_1 = 1 \\
b_0 = -1 \qquad
b_1 = 0 \\
c_0 = -1 \qquad
c_1 = 0 \\
v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2 = 1 \qquad
v_4 = 1
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

is a solution of \eqref{schrodinger hydrogen 2} under these conditions:

\begin{equation}
\label{related solution}
\begin{gathered}

v \Psi'' + \Psi' + \Psi = 0 \\
v = v_1 x+ v_2 y+ v_3 z+r \\
v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2 = 1
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The expression $v_1 x + v_2 y + v_3 z$ is easily identified as a dot product between
the coordinate $(x,y,z)$ and the unit vector $(v_1, v_2, v_3)$ (remembering
that $v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2 = 1$).  The direction of this vector is arbitrary,
so we can orient the $x$-axis in this direction and set $(v_1, v_2, v_3) = (1,0,0)$
without loss of generality.

We now have to solve a second order ODE:

\begin{equation}
v \Psi''(v) + \Psi'(v) + \Psi(v) = 0
\end{equation}

\Psi = J_0(2\sqrt{x+r})
\end{equation}

where $x,y,z$ are Cartesian coordinates, $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, $E=0$,
and $J_0$ is the ordinary Bessel function $J_0$.

produce it.  It is, however, separable in {\it parabolic} coordinates: with
$\xi = r + x$ one of the parabolic coordinates, \eqref{solution} is a function

(\cite{LandauLifshitz}, \S37).  This is no accident, and it is illuminated by
the two branches the algorithm returned.  The classical branch
\eqref{ideal:1}, with $v = r$, is the spherical separation; the present branch
\eqref{ideal:2}, with $v = v_1 x + v_2 y + v_3 z + v_4 r$, is the parabolic
one.  That the hydrogen atom separates in {\it both} systems is the signature
of its hidden symmetry: beyond the angular momentum $\mathbf{L}$, the Coulomb
problem conserves the Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector $\mathbf{A}$, and together
$\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ generate the dynamical $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ symmetry
responsible for the degeneracy of the bound spectrum
(\cite{BanderItzykson}).  Spherical separation diagonalizes $\{L^2, L_z\}$;
parabolic separation about an axis $\hat{a}$ diagonalizes instead the
components $\mathbf{A}\cdot\hat{a}$ and $\mathbf{L}\cdot\hat{a}$.  The algorithm
recovers {\it both} separations from a single ansatz, without committing to any
coordinate system in advance --- and the unit vector fixing the parabolic axis
in the generalized solution \eqref{generalized solution} is precisely the
direction of the Runge--Lenz vector.
\end{comment}

\subsection{Physical Interpretation}
\label{sec:physical}

Since \eqref{solution} is a solution to a simple Schr\"odinger equation for hydrogen,
it is reasonable to ask if it has any physical interpretation.  (It does not.)

In order to be a physically realistic solution, a function must both solve the Schr\"odinger equation
and be a valid wavefunction.  For a given wavefunction $\Psi$, the modulus squared $|\Psi|^2$ is a probability density function
for the position of the electron (\cite{pauling and wilson} \S 10a),
%and must have a finite integral,
so it follows that a valid
wavefunction must be in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$, since its total probability must be finite.

The failure to be square-integrable is structural, not accidental.  In the
parabolic coordinates $\xi = r + x$, $\eta = r - x$ in which \eqref{solution}
separates, our solution is a function of $\xi$ alone; this forces the $\eta$
separation parameter to vanish, which in turn pins the energy to $E = 0$
(\cite{LandauLifshitz}, \S37), the threshold between the discrete ($E < 0$) and
continuous ($E > 0$) spectra.  The square-integrable bound states depend on
{\it both} parabolic coordinates --- each contributing a decaying confluent
hypergeometric factor --- and occur only at the discrete energies
$E_n = -1/2n^2$.  A solution that is a function of a single combination $v$,
and hence of a single parabolic coordinate, therefore cannot be one of them:
it necessarily lands on the non-normalizable threshold.

This situation is not unusual.  Classical solutions to hydrogen exist (as hypergeometric series) for all energy values,
but these solutions are only in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$ for specific values of $E$;
only these wavefunctions are observed as atomic orbitals.

\subsection{Generalization of the Example}
\parskip 12pt

The choice of $x$ is arbitrary, and any ordinary Bessel function can be used:

\begin{equation}
\label{generalized solution}
\Psi = F(2\sqrt{a_1 x+ a_2 y+ a_3 z+r})
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation*}
a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2=1
\end{equation*}

and $F$ is any linear combination of the Bessel functions $J_0$ and $Y_0$.

Any finite linear combination of functions of the form \eqref{generalized solution} also solves \eqref{schrodinger hydrogen 2}.

Futhermore, since wavefunctions are complex-valued, restricting the $v_i$ coefficients
to be real is unnecessary.

A deeper generalization would enlarge the ansatz itself.  The solutions found
here are functions of a single combination $v$, hence of a single parabolic
coordinate, and are for that reason confined to the $E = 0$ threshold
(\S\ref{sec:physical}).  An ansatz built from {\it two} ODE-described factors,
$\Psi = f_1(v_1)\,f_2(v_2)$ --- one for each parabolic coordinate --- would
instead span the product structure of the square-integrable bound states,
whose factors are confluent hypergeometric functions of $\xi = r + x$ and
$\eta = r - x$ (\cite{LandauLifshitz}, \S37).  Recovering the discrete spectrum
$E_n = -1/2n^2$ in this way, with the algorithm discovering the two-coordinate
parabolic structure rather than presupposing it, is a natural direction for
future work.

\section{Example: Incompressible Navier--Stokes}
\label{sec:NavierStokes}

The hydrogen example ran the simplest ansatz of Section \ref{sec:Ansatze}
against a single linear PDE.  We now run the {\it last} one --- the nonlinear,
second-degree ansatz of Figure \ref{nonlinear ansatz figure}, whose four
top-level polynomials $u,v,w,p$ were introduced precisely to serve as the
dependent variables of a system --- against the incompressible Navier--Stokes
equations.

Where the derivation below lands is worth having in view from the start.  It
lands on the {\it normalized truncation} of that ansatz drawn in Figure
\ref{normalized ansatz figure}: the ODE has been truncated to its linear part,
the six second-degree jet monomials dropped, and the symmetries of the problem
have been used up to cut the twenty-eight constants of Figure
\ref{nonlinear ansatz figure} (with the linear backgrounds of $u,v,w$ removed)
down to nine.  Rotations of $(x,y,z)$ put the covector $\mathbf{s}$ along $x$
and the amplitude $\mathbf{a}$ in the $x$--$y$ plane, so that $s_2 = s_3 = 0$
and $w \equiv 0$; the three scalings the ansatz admits --- multiplying the ODE
by a constant, scaling $\Psi$, scaling $s$ --- are pinned by $g_0 = 1$,
$v_5 = 1$ and $s_1 = 1$; and the mean pressure gradient is set to zero,
$p_1 = p_2 = p_3 = 0$.  What is left is

\begin{equation}
\label{ansatz 18 normalized}
\begin{gathered}
\Psi_c = \Psi' s_c , \qquad \Psi'_c = \Psi'' s_c \qquad (c = x,y,z,t) \\
(1 + g_1 s)\,\Psi'' + (h_0 + h_1 s)\,\Psi' + (i_0 + i_1 s)\,\Psi = 0 \\
s = x + s_4 t \\
u = u_5 \Psi , \qquad v = \Psi , \qquad w = 0 , \qquad p = p_4 t + p_5 \Psi
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\noindent
in the nine constants $s_4$; $g_1, h_0, h_1, i_0, i_1$; $u_5$; $p_4, p_5$.
Figure \ref{normalized ansatz figure} draws \eqref{ansatz 18 normalized} in the
notation of Figure \ref{key}: there are nine white boxes, one for each of those
constants.  The three coefficients of the ODE are first degree polynomials in
$s$ and their lines are labelled accordingly, the leading one differing from
the others only in having had its constant term normalized to $1$.  The
remaining lines carry no degree bound, because the polynomials they belong to
are confined to part of their ring --- $s$ to two of the four coordinates,
$u,v,w,p$ to $\Psi$ and $t$ --- and are written out instead; $v$ and $w$, fixed
outright, carry no box at all.

None of these reductions is free, and each is earned below rather than
assumed: the truncation to a linear ODE is the cell $a_0 = a_1 = 0$ that the
degree mismatch of \S\ref{ns viscous block} forces on any viscous solution,
and the vanishing of the mean pressure gradient is a conclusion of
\S\ref{ns shear layer}, not a hypothesis.  Two cautions attach to the picture.
The rotation used here differs from the one used by hand in
\S\ref{ns shear layer}, which sends $\mathbf{s}$ along $z$ and $\mathbf{a}$
along $x$; the two describe the same solution in different coordinates.  And
truncating the ODE, unlike the rotation and the scalings, is not a change of
chart but a change of family: Figure \ref{normalized ansatz figure} is a
smaller ansatz, and what it finds is not automatically a result about Figure
\ref{nonlinear ansatz figure} until the second-degree block is separately shown
to be inert, which is what \S\ref{ns viscous block} does.

The purpose of the example is not to produce a new solution of Navier--Stokes.
It is that this particular pairing of ansatz and PDE reduces in closed form,
by hand, and that the closed form says something sharp about which solutions
the ansatz can reach and, more interestingly, why it cannot reach the others.
Two obstructions appear, and neither is visible from the ansatz diagram alone:
the nonlinearity of the equations is annihilated by their own incompressibility
condition, and the viscous term is blocked by a mismatch of degrees between
the ansatz's ODE and the PDE.

\subsection{The equations}

For constant density $\rho$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu$ (kinematic viscosity
$\nu = \mu/\rho$), the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations are

\begin{subequations}
\label{navier stokes}
\begin{gather}
u_x + v_y + w_z = 0 \label{navier stokes:continuity} \\
\rho\,(u_t + u u_x + v u_y + w u_z) + p_x - \mu\,(u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}) = 0 \label{navier stokes:x} \\
\rho\,(v_t + u v_x + v v_y + w v_z) + p_y - \mu\,(v_{xx}+v_{yy}+v_{zz}) = 0 \label{navier stokes:y} \\
\rho\,(w_t + u w_x + v w_y + w w_z) + p_z - \mu\,(w_{xx}+w_{yy}+w_{zz}) = 0 \label{navier stokes:z}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}

Unlike the hydrogen equation \eqref{schrodinger hydrogen 2}, these require no
clearing of denominators.  Once $u,v,w,p$ are replaced by the polynomials of
\eqref{ansatz 18} they are already differential polynomials in

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ns-ring}
R \;=\; \mathbb{Q}[x,y,z,t]\{\,\Psi, \Psi', \Psi'', s, u, v, w, p,\;
   \rho, \mu, a_0, \ldots, i_1, s_1, \ldots, s_4, u_1, \ldots, p_5 \,\}
\end{equation}

\noindent
with $\Delta = \{\delta_x, \delta_y, \delta_z, \delta_t\}$.  As with $E$ in the
hydrogen example, nothing compels us to treat $\rho$ and $\mu$ as given; they
are ranked in the lowest block alongside the constants of the ansatz, and the
membership locus is free to constrain them.  It will do exactly that below.

\subsection{Reduction}

Because $s$ is linear in the coordinates, its derivatives $s_x = s_1$,
$s_y = s_2$, $s_z = s_3$ and $s_t = s_4$ are constants, and the chain rules of
\eqref{ansatz 18} collapse the first derivatives of each field to

\begin{equation}
\label{ns first derivatives}
u_x = u_1 + u_5 s_1 \Psi' \quad
u_y = u_2 + u_5 s_2 \Psi' \quad
u_z = u_3 + u_5 s_3 \Psi' \quad
u_t = u_4 + u_5 s_4 \Psi'
\end{equation}

\noindent
and likewise for $v$, $w$ and $p$ with their own coefficients.  Applying the
chain rules a second time, every Laplacian in \eqref{navier stokes} collapses
to a single term,

\begin{equation}
\label{ns laplacian}
u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz} \;=\; u_5\,\sigma\,\Psi'' ,
\qquad \text{where} \quad \sigma \;=\; s_1^2+s_2^2+s_3^2 .
\end{equation}

It is convenient to name two vectors of constants,

\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{s} = (s_1,s_2,s_3), \qquad \mathbf{a} = (u_5,v_5,w_5),
\end{equation*}

\noindent
$\mathbf{s}$ being the spatial part of the covector that defines the ODE's
independent variable and $\mathbf{a}$ the amplitude with which the ODE element
enters the velocity field, and to write $U,V,W,P$ for the parts of $u,v,w,p$
that are linear in the coordinates, so that $u = U + u_5 \Psi$,
$p = P + p_5\Psi$ and $\mathbf{\bar{v}} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{a}\Psi$ with
$\mathbf{L} = (U,V,W)$.

Substituting into \eqref{navier stokes} and collecting on the jets gives the
reduced system

\begin{subequations}
\label{ns reduced}
\begin{gather}
(u_1+v_2+w_3) \;+\; (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s})\,\Psi' \label{ns reduced:continuity} \\
\begin{aligned}
&\rho\bigl[\partial_t \mathbf{L} + (\mathbf{L}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{L}\bigr] + \nabla P
   \;+\; \rho\,\bigl[(\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{L}\bigr]\,\Psi \\
&\quad {} + \bigl[\rho s_4\,\mathbf{a} + \rho(\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{a}
   + p_5\,\mathbf{s}\bigr]\,\Psi'
   \;+\; \rho\,(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{a}\,\Psi\Psi'
   \;-\; \mu\sigma\,\mathbf{a}\,\Psi''
\end{aligned} \label{ns reduced:momentum}
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}

\noindent
the three momentum residuals being the three components of
\eqref{ns reduced:momentum}.  Its $x$ component, for instance, has jet-free
part $\rho u_4 + p_1 + \rho(u_1 U + u_2 V + u_3 W)$ and $\Psi$ coefficient
$\rho(u_1 u_5 + u_2 v_5 + u_3 w_5)$; note that the $u_5$ there is a component
of $\mathbf{a}$ and stays put in the $y$ and $z$ components, where the
gradient row $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ becomes $(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ and $(w_1,w_2,w_3)$.
Every jet monomial appearing anywhere in \eqref{ns reduced} is drawn from the
five-element set $\{1,\,\Psi,\,\Psi',\,\Psi\Psi',\,\Psi''\}$.  Nothing else
occurs --- in particular no $\Psi''^2$, no $\Psi''\Psi'$ and no $\Psi'^2$.

\subsection{The convective nonlinearity}

Navier--Stokes is nonlinear only through the convective derivative
$(\mathbf{\bar{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\bar{v}}$.  Under this ansatz the velocity
field is a fixed direction $\mathbf{a}$ modulated by a single scalar profile
$\Psi$ of a single scalar argument $s$, superposed on a background $\mathbf{L}$
linear in the coordinates.  Expanding the $x$ component of the convective
derivative in those terms separates it into four pieces:

\begin{equation}
\label{convective split}
\begin{gathered}
(\mathbf{\bar{v}}\cdot\nabla)u \;=\;
\underbrace{(\mathbf{L}\cdot\nabla U)}_{\text{background--background}}
\;+\; \underbrace{(\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla U)\,\Psi}_{\text{wave--background}} \\[6pt]
{} \;+\; \underbrace{u_5(\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{s})\,\Psi'}_{\text{background--wave}}
\;+\; \underbrace{u_5(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s})\,\Psi\Psi'}_{\text{wave--wave}}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

Only the last piece is quadratic in the jets, and it is the only quadratic jet
monomial in the entire system.  Its coefficient carries the factor
$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s}$ --- which is precisely the coefficient of $\Psi'$
in the continuity residual \eqref{ns reduced:continuity}.

The consequence is immediate, but worth stating carefully, because it is a
property of the ansatz and not an approximation.  The decomposition splits on
$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s}$.  On the cells where continuity holds with a
non-degenerate $\Psi'$ we have $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} = 0$, and then the
wave--wave term of \eqref{convective split} vanishes identically --- in all
three momentum equations at once, since their coefficients are
$\rho u_5 (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s})$, $\rho v_5 (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s})$
and $\rho w_5 (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s})$.  What is left is linear in
$\Psi$, $\Psi'$ and $\Psi''$.

The condition $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} = 0$ has a clean reading: the amplitude
of the wave is orthogonal to the direction in which the wave varies.  The field
is transverse --- it is a shear wave, and the profile varies only along a
direction in which the velocity does not point.  A fluid dynamicist would
recognize the conclusion, since unidirectional flow has no self-advection, but
here it is not assumed.  It is one of the branches the decomposition returns.

Two qualifications keep this from being stronger than it is.  First, only the
wave's self-interaction is destroyed.  The background--wave term
$u_5(\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{s})\Psi'$ survives; it is linear in the jets, but
its coefficient is linear in $x,y,z,t$, so it contributes several distinct
equations to the constant locus, and the example is not a linear problem in
disguise.  Second, the jet-free row of \eqref{convective split} contributes
$(\mathbf{L}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{L} = 0$ up to the pressure's linear part, the
familiar requirement that the background be one of the linear
strain-and-rotation flows.

What this says about the ansatz is that its second-degree monomials are not
being asked for by this PDE.  Figure \ref{nonlinear ansatz figure} was drawn
with $\Psi''^2$, $\Psi''\Psi'$, $\Psi''\Psi$, $\Psi'^2$, $\Psi'\Psi$ and
$\Psi^2$ all available, and the natural reading of the figure is that the
ansatz's nonlinearity is there to meet Navier--Stokes' nonlinearity.  It is
not.  The residual's only quadratic term is $\Psi\Psi'$, and incompressibility
removes it.  A second-degree ODE buys a wider class of profile functions
$\Psi$, which is a different thing --- and as the next subsection shows, one of
those six monomials actively costs us something.

\subsection{How the viscous term is blocked}
\label{ns viscous block}

The Laplacian is the only source of $\Psi''$ in \eqref{ns reduced}.  It enters
each momentum residual exactly once, through \eqref{ns laplacian}, and it enters
linearly.  Each residual is therefore of degree {\it one} in $\Psi''$.  The
ansatz's ODE, on the other hand, is of degree {\it two} in $\Psi''$, on account
of its $(a_0 + a_1 s)\Psi''^2$ term.

$\Psi''$ is the leader of both under any ranking that places the jets above the
constants and orders $\Psi'' \gg \Psi' \gg \Psi$.  Ritt's reduction
(\S\ref{sec:preliminaries}) can lower the degree of a differential polynomial
$r$ in the leader of $A$ only when $\deg_{\Psi''} r \ge \deg_{\Psi''} A$, and
here $1 < 2$.  Nor does full reduction rescue the situation: the proper
derivatives of $A$ introduce $\Psi'''$, and the residuals contain no $\Psi'''$
at all.  Each momentum residual is thus {\it already fully reduced} modulo the
ansatz.  Pseudo-division returns it unchanged, and the ODE --- the entire
content of the figure's orange box --- contributes nothing whatever to the
reduction.

The algorithm therefore passes the residual to the projection step untouched,
and the projection sets each of its coefficients to zero separately.  The
$\Psi''$ coefficients give

\begin{equation}
\label{viscous block}
\mu u_5 \sigma \;=\; \mu v_5 \sigma \;=\; \mu w_5 \sigma \;=\; 0 .
\end{equation}

If $\mu \ne 0$, then either $\sigma = 0$ or $\mathbf{a} = 0$.  Over the reals
$\sigma = s_1^2+s_2^2+s_3^2 = 0$ forces $\mathbf{s} = 0$, so that $s = s_4 t$
and the profile has no spatial dependence whatever, while $\mathbf{a} = 0$
removes $\Psi$ from the velocity field altogether and leaves only the linear
background.  On this branch, then, Navier--Stokes admits nothing but inviscid
or spatially degenerate solutions.

It is worth being precise about the culprit, because it is easy to read
\eqref{viscous block} as a statement about fluids.  It is not.  The obstruction
is that a PDE of degree one in a leader cannot be reduced by an ansatz of
degree two in that same leader.  The higher degree does not add solutions; it
makes the ansatz inert.

The remedy is already inside the algorithm.  The Thomas decomposition of the
ansatz splits on the initial of its ODE, so the locus $a_0 = a_1 = 0$ is one of
the cells the decomposition produces, not something we have to impose by hand.
On that cell the ODE reads

\begin{equation}
\label{ns reducible ode}
\begin{gathered}
\bigl[(b_0+b_1 s)\Psi' + (c_0+c_1 s)\Psi + (g_0+g_1 s)\bigr]\Psi'' \\
{} + (d_0+d_1 s)\Psi'^2 + (e_0+e_1 s)\Psi'\Psi + (f_0+f_1 s)\Psi^2 \\
{} + (h_0+h_1 s)\Psi' + (i_0+i_1 s)\Psi \;=\; 0
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\noindent
which is of degree one in $\Psi''$, and pseudo-division by it now does
something.  Note how little has to be given up.  Of the six second-degree
monomials only $\Psi''^2$ is switched off; the other five survive, and the two
that involve the leader, $\Psi''\Psi'$ and $\Psi''\Psi$, are positively useful,
in that they make the initial of \eqref{ns reducible ode} a non-constant
differential polynomial on which the decomposition splits again, so that the
degenerate loci are reported as cells of their own rather than silently
excluded.

Stated generally, this is a criterion for the selection of an ansatz
(\S\ref{sec:Selection of an Ansatz}): the degree of the ansatz's defining
polynomial in a given leader should not exceed the degree in which that leader
occurs in the PDE, or the ansatz cannot act on the PDE at all.

\subsection{The traveling-wave shear layer}
\label{ns shear layer}

Work on the cell $a_0 = a_1 = 0$, and take the linear background to vanish,
$\mathbf{L} = 0$, so that $u = u_5\Psi$, $v = v_5\Psi$, $w = w_5\Psi$ and
$p = p_1 x + p_2 y + p_3 z + p_4 t + p_5 \Psi$.  The continuity residual
\eqref{ns reduced:continuity} then reduces to $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} = 0$
alone.  Since $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{s}$ are both unknowns of the problem we
may rotate coordinates freely; choose $\mathbf{s}$ along $z$ and $\mathbf{a}$
along $x$, so that

\begin{equation*}
s = s_3 z + s_4 t, \qquad \mathbf{a} = (u_5,0,0), \qquad
\sigma = s_3^2, \qquad \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} = 0 .
\end{equation*}

The $y$ and $z$ momentum residuals then have $v \equiv w \equiv 0$ and collapse
to $p_2 = 0$ and $p_3 + p_5 s_3 \Psi' = 0$, whence $p_3 = 0$ and $p_5 = 0$: the
pressure carries no part of the ODE element.  The $x$ residual is what remains,

\begin{equation}
\label{ns shear residual}
p_1 \;+\; \rho u_5 s_4\,\Psi' \;-\; \mu u_5 s_3^2\,\Psi'' \;=\; 0 .
\end{equation}

Its jet-free coefficient gives $p_1 = 0$, no mean pressure gradient --- the
specialization to $u_4 = 0$ of the general jet-free condition
$p_1 = -\rho u_4$, in which a uniformly accelerating background balances a
pressure gradient.  What is left,

\begin{equation}
\label{ns shear ode}
\mu s_3^2\,\Psi'' - \rho s_4\,\Psi' \;=\; 0 ,
\end{equation}

is not a further constraint but the ansatz's own ODE.  Matching it against
\eqref{ns reducible ode} fixes $b_* = c_* = d_* = e_* = f_* = i_* = 0$ and
$g_1 = h_1 = 0$, with

\begin{equation*}
\frac{h_0}{g_0} \;=\; -\,\frac{\rho s_4}{\mu s_3^2} .
\end{equation*}

The membership locus is therefore nonempty.  The reduction does not annihilate
the ansatz's coefficients; it pins them.

Integrating \eqref{ns shear ode} gives the solution

\begin{equation}
\label{ns shear solution}
\Psi' \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(\frac{\rho s_4}{\mu s_3^2}\,s\right),
\qquad u = u_5\,\Psi(s_3 z + s_4 t), \qquad v = w = 0 ,
\end{equation}

\noindent
with the pressure a function of $t$ alone, which is to say an additive gauge.
Writing $c = -s_4/s_3$ for the speed at which the profile translates and
$\zeta = z - ct$ for the co-moving coordinate, so that $s = s_3 \zeta$,
\eqref{ns shear solution} takes the form

\begin{equation}
\label{ns stokes layer}
u(\zeta) \;=\; A + B\,e^{-c\zeta/\nu} , \qquad \nu = \mu/\rho ,
\end{equation}

\noindent
an exponential shear layer of thickness $\nu/c$ propagating rigidly along $z$
at speed $c$.  This is the traveling-wave member of the Rayleigh--Stokes family
of viscous shear solutions: a front separating two streams, held at a fixed
thickness by the balance between viscous diffusion, which thickens it, and the
translation, which sweeps vorticity through it.  In profile it is the same
exponential as the asymptotic suction boundary layer.  That
\eqref{ns stokes layer} solves \eqref{navier stokes} exactly is immediate --- the
convective term vanishes identically because the flow is unidirectional with
$u_x = 0$, which is \eqref{convective split} with $\mathbf{L} = 0$ and
$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{s} = 0$.

It is instructive that the {\it original} Rayleigh problem is not in this
family.  Stokes' first problem, the impulsively started plate, has the
self-similar solution $u = U\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(z/2\sqrt{\nu t}\bigr)$, a
function of $z/\sqrt{t}$.  This ansatz cannot express it, and the reason is
visible in the diagram rather than in the analysis: the ODE's independent
variable $s$ is drawn as a first-degree polynomial in the base ring
$\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z,t]$, and $z/\sqrt{t}$ is not a polynomial in $x,y,z,t$ at all.
Reaching it would mean adjoining an algebraic element $\tau$ with
$\tau^2 = t$ and defining the independent variable by the cleared relation
$\tau s - z$ --- the algebraic-extension mechanism of Figure
\ref{ansatz 11 figure}, applied to the base rather than to the ODE's
coefficients.

\subsection{What the example shows}

The machinery of \S\ref{sec:Solution Algorithm} runs unchanged on a nonlinear
system of four PDEs in four dependent variables, and its output is informative
even where the answer is largely negative.  The solutions it returns are
exactly the parallel shear flows, and the two reasons it returns nothing else
are structural.

Removing the second obstruction costs one monomial: drop $\Psi''^2$, or equally
well work on the cell $a_0 = a_1 = 0$ that the decomposition hands us anyway.
Removing the first is a deeper change.  As long as $u$, $v$ and $w$ are affine
in one common ODE element $\Psi$, the velocity is a single profile times a
fixed direction, and $(\mathbf{\bar{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\bar{v}}$ cannot
survive incompressibility.  Letting the components carry {\it different} ODE
elements --- several extensions adjoined in parallel, in the sense of
\S\ref{sec:Generalization of the Algorithm} --- is what an ansatz would need in
order to reach a flow whose nonlinearity is active.

\section{Generalization of the Algorithm}
\label{sec:Generalization of the Algorithm}

The algorithm of \S\ref{sec:Solution Algorithm} already accepts a system of any
size --- that is what the loop of lines~4--7 is for --- so the generalizations
that remain are on the other side of the computation, in the ansatz.  A
collection of sample ans\"atze was presented in Section \ref{sec:Ansatze}; this
section sketches how much further they can be pushed.

Multiple ODE extensions can be introduced, either in parallel, allowing the ODE solutions to be combined together into a polynomial
that solves the PDE, or in series (Figure \ref{ansatz 12 figure}), allowing either the independent variable of one ODE
to be formed from the solutions to a second ODE.  Extensions can also be introduced in the coefficient
ring of the ODE (Figures \ref{ansatz 17 figure} and \ref{ansatz 13 figure})

In addition to ODE extensions, algebraic extensions can be introduced either explicitly or implicitly.
Figure \ref{ansatz 11 figure} shows an explicit second degree algebraic extension.
Introducing higher degree algebraic extensions is also possible.
Figure \ref{ansatz 16 figure}, a somewhat simpler variant on the same idea, shows an ansatz that introduces a square root,
then allows the ODE's independent variable to be selected from the resulting
algebraic extension.

Parameterized polynomials of any (finite) degree can be used in lieu of the linear polynomials.  Figure \ref{ansatz 16 figure}
illustrates the use of second degree polynomials.  Polynomials can be further restricted.  Perhaps we have some
theoretical consideration which suggests that the independent variable should be symmetric.

Nonlinear ODEs can be introduced, as shown in Figure \ref{ansatz 17 figure}.

Nonlinear ODEs permit algebraic extensions to be introduced implicitly.  Consider that for a given algebraic extension:

\begin{equation}
f_n(x) y^n + \cdots + f_1(x) y + f_0(x) \qquad f_i(x) \in \mathbb{C}[x]
\end{equation}

we can form the derivative with respect to $x$ as follows:

\begin{equation}
y' = \frac{f'_n(x) y^n + \cdots + f'_1(x) y + f'_0(x)}{n f_n(x) y^{n-1} + \cdots + f_1(x)}
\end{equation}

This is a non-linear ODE that is first order, $n^{\rm th}$ degree in $y$.

Note that if we limit our ansatz to linear ODEs, then any algebraic extensions need
to be introduced explicitly; i.e, a linear ODE will not match algebraic extensions.

We can introduce inhomogenous ODEs very similarly to the nonlinear case by
introducing a zeroth degree coefficient into the differential equation.

Do we need to restrict the coefficients of the ODE to a ring generated by the independent variable?
We could allow coefficients in the ODE to involve indeterminates other than $v$, and there
are two major cases to consider.  The first case is when we can partition the indeterminates
into two sets, one used to express the independent variable,
and one used to express the coefficients of the ODE.  This yields a
{\it parameterized ODE}, one whose coefficients vary according to one set of independent variables (the parameters),
and whose independent variable depended on a different set of them.
The parameters can be treated as constants for the purpose of solving the ODE.
I don't know of any way to enforce this requirement
other than having multiple ans\"atze, each with a different partitioning of the indeterminates.
One large ansatz could be constructed that would enforce nothing, but would still allow for ODE solutions of the form described next.

What if the dependencies of the ODE coefficients and the ODE variable can't be partitioned into two different sets of variables?

Then we're going to get an ODE something like this (two dimensional, for illustrative purposes):

\begin{equation}
\label{weird ODE}
f(u,v) \frac{d^2\Psi}{dv^2} + g(u,v) \frac{d\Psi}{dv} + h(u,v) \Psi = 0
\end{equation}

the point being that we can't separate the $v$ dependency out of the coefficients.

I'm not aware of any theory to handle an ODE of the form \eqref{weird ODE}, but one might exist,
or be developed.  Would it be useful?  Perhaps.  \eqref{weird ODE} is still simpler than a PDE because
it only involves one derivative.  The algorithm described in this paper could test for the existence
of such solutions.  I'm somewhat skeptical of their utility.

\section{Selection of an Ansatz}
\label{sec:Selection of an Ansatz}

The most obvious question remains: how to select an ansatz?

First, can we build any such ansatz to express all of the solutions of the PDE?
This seems unlikely, as the solutions of a PDE would include solutions for all possible
boundary conditions.

For example, the simple heat equation $u_t = u_{xx}$ admits an infinity of solutions of the form

\begin{equation}
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{4t}} \qquad \forall x_0\in\mathbb{R}
\end{equation}

No single exponential extension can be constructed to express all of these solutions.
In fact, a different extension is required for each real value of $x_0$, yielding a
uncountable infinite basis.

Furthermore, these are just the solutions described in a series expansion;
we expect there to be many others.  The essence of the problem of ansatz selection
is that we expect there to be solutions all over the place.  Many ans\"atze
will yield solutions.

So, while the PDE problem is superficially similar to the problem of symbolic integration,
which asks if a finite tower of extensions can be used to express an integral,
the nature of the problem is quite different because in the integration case we can express all of its solutions
in a single extension.  In fact, given one solution to the integration problem, we know that all of its solutions
are related by adding a constant $C$, which implies that a field containing one solution contains all solutions.
In the symbolic integration case, we expect to find a single field extension that contains all solutions,
while in the PDE case we expect this to be impossible.

What about differential Galois theory?

Picard-Vessiot / differential Galois theory has been of great utility in analyzing ordinary differential equations.
An $n^{\rm th}$ order linear ODE,
% restricted to analytic solutions (complex? what function space?),
will have at most an $n$-dimensional
solution space.  After selecting an arbitrary basis, any
solution can be represented by an $n$-tuple of complex constants.
Any automorphism that permutes these solutions can be represented by
an $n \times n$ matrix, so differential Galois theory can be reduced
to the analysis of $GL_n(\mathbb{C})$, which has facilitated the work of Kovacic, Singer, and Bronstein.

No such easy reduction is available for PDEs.
A PDE with no boundary conditions
is likely to have a solution space that is not only infinite, but infinite
dimensional, and probably not even with a countably infinite basis.
In such an environment, differential Galois groups
can not be represented as subgroups of $GL_n$,
so the theory of algebraic groups is therefore unlikely to be much help.

Kovacic developed an algorithm \cite{Kovacic} to handle
second order ODEs not by developing an algorithm to determine the
differential Galois group of a given equation, but rather by
using first a change of variables to restrict the Galois group to $SL_2$, then
categorizing all possible algebraic subgroups of $SL_2$
and developing an algorithm to handle each case.
Taking a similar approach for PDEs would require categorizing
all possible linear mappings between function spaces,
which does not seem feasible.

% We'd have to characterize the solution we seek.  Conditions like lowest energy level don't seem
% useful because they require evaluating a global integral.

In short, the differential algebra techniques used to analyze integrals and linear ODEs do not seem amenable
to handling PDEs, and no theory is available at this time to guide the selection of an ansatz.
The ansatz used for the hydrogen example
was an educated guess.  It was designed to be general enough to find the existing
classical solution, and so to be used for testing the software.  The appearance of \eqref{ideal:2}
was not anticipated, as the solutions was previously unknown,
and serves to illustrate the mysterious nature of these ans\"atze.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:Future Work}

The difficulties of selecting an ansatz have already be touched upon.
One possible approach to ansatz selection would be to impose boundary
conditions (or the like) prior to any kind of analysis with differential algebra.
In the case of quantum mechanics, imposing $H^2(\Omega)$, where $\Omega$ is the state space
of whatever physical system we're analyzing, is an obvious restriction.
Some kind of theoretical analysis, exploring what restrictions must be placed
on ans\"atze to force the functions into $H^2(\Omega)$, might yield
useful techniques to better select ans\"atze.

What relationship is there between the constant ideals and the function space?

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:Conclusion}

The algorithm presented above can be used to check any PDE to see if any of its solutions
can be expressed using an ODE structured according to a specific ansatz, defined by
differential polynomials and parameterized by a finite number of constants.
This technique
is complementary to separation of variables, where we check a PDE to see if any of
its solutions can be expressed as a product of factors, each depending on only
a single variable, with no restrictions to differential polynomials.
It is likewise complementary to the {\it similarity-reduction} methods --- the
classical Lie symmetry method \cite{Olver}, the Clarkson--Kruskal direct method
\cite{ClarksonKruskal,ClarksonMansfield}, and the nonclassical (conditional) symmetries of Bluman
and Cole \cite{BlumanCole} --- which also reduce a PDE to an ODE without assuming
a separable product form, but which are organized around the (classical or
conditional) symmetries of the equation.  Our reductions are instead organized
around the differential-algebraic structure of the ansatz; they do not
presuppose any symmetry of the PDE, and they carry the completeness guarantee of
Theorem~\ref{thm:completeness}.

The name of the software repository ({\tt helium}) informs a primary goal
of my research: to find a closed form expression for helium's ground state.
In that repository, you will find several years of attempts in
that direction, of which {\tt joca.sage} is the most refined.
% \footnote{{\tt helium.sage} is also notable for containing a very aggressive, though inefficient,
% factorizing implementation of the GTZ algorithm, but that's the subject of another paper.}
Although ``solving helium'' is widely regarded as impossible, I know
of no proof that helium's ground state wavefunction can not be formed
from a finite number of linear homogenous ODEs.  Lacking any good theory
to inform the selection of an ansatz, I have turned to educated guesswork.

We can explore other important PDEs, such as the Navier-Stokes equation.
As with helium, we can work incrementally towards it using smaller ans\"atze.
Ansatz \eqref{ansatz 18} is a step in the direction
of analyzing Navier-Stokes.  $\mathbf{\bar{v}} = (u,v,w)$ is meant to be a velocity field
and $p$ is the pressure, all varying as functions of position $(x,y,z)$ and time $t$.

We can solve differential equations with Gr\"obner bases, and this
technique, given enough compute resources, allows us to check for simple ODE-based solutions,
but we're primarily limited by inefficiencies of our software.

As my primary interest lies in quantum mechanics, I have investigated the PDEs
that model hydrogen and helium.

For hydrogen, the PDE is $\nabla^2 \Psi - \frac{1}{r} \Psi = E \Psi$

For helium, the PDE is $\nabla_1^2 \Psi + \nabla_2^2 \Psi - \frac{2}{r_1} - \Psi \frac{2}{r_2} - \Psi \frac{1}{r_{12}} \Psi = E \Psi$,
where $\Psi=\Psi(r_1,r_2,r_{12})$ and $\nabla_i$ is the Laplacian with respect to the $i^{\rm th}$ electron.
$\Psi$ is assumed to have no angular dependence, which has been known since
at least the time of Hylleraas to be a valid assumption for the ground state.

In both cases, I use Hartree atomic units to render the equations dimensionless.
